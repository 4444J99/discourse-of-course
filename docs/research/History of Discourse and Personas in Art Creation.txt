The Architecture of Aesthetic
Legitimation: A History of Art
Meta-Discourse and Rhetorical Personas
from Antiquity to the Algorithmic Age
Theoretical Foundations of Art Meta-Discourse and
Persona Construction
The history of art is only secondarily a history of physical artifacts; primarily, it is a history of the
linguistic, institutional, and rhetorical frameworks that make aesthetics—"understood as a
discourse about art"—possible in the first place. Across millennia, a physical object only crosses
the ontological boundary from mere matter or manual craft into "art" when it is enveloped by a
meta-discourse: a second-order system of treatises, biographies, critical reviews, manifestos,
curatorial statements, and algorithmic paratexts that foregrounds language, codifies evaluative
methodologies, and integrates visual production into broader structures of thought, perception,
and society. To analyze how this meta-discourse operates independently of the art object,
modern scholarship relies on three intersecting theoretical pillars: Gérard Genette's theory of
paratextuality, Pierre Bourdieu's sociology of symbolic goods, and rhetorical persona theory.
In Paratexts: Thresholds of Interpretation (Seuils, 1987/1997), Gérard Genette demonstrates
that an artistic or literary work rarely appears in a naked state; rather, it is surrounded and
prolonged by verbal and non-verbal productions that ensure its presence in the world, its
reception, and its consumption. Genette defines this mediating apparatus as the paratext,
characterizing it not as a sealed frontier but as an "undecided zone" and a "vestibule" between
the inside (the work itself) and the outside (the discourse of the world on the work). As Philippe
Lejeune observed, this fringe of the work "in reality, controls the whole reading," functioning as a
privileged site of pragmatics, strategy, and transaction with the public. Genette subdivides the
paratext into two spatial categories based on their material proximity to the work. The first
category, the peritext, encompasses elements located in immediate proximity to the primary
object, such as signatures, titles, frames, dedications, epigraphs, and museum wall labels. The
second category, the epitext, comprises distanced discursive elements not materially appended
to the work—including workshop treatises, biographies, Salon reviews, manifestos, artist
interviews, academic lectures, YouTube video essays, and TikTok process reels—that govern
production, promotion, dissemination, reception, and extension. Crucially, Genette notes that
much of the authorial epitext, particularly interviews, conversations, and confidences, is officious
rather than official, allowing the creator to strategically shape public interpretation while retaining
plausible deniability through convenient disavowals such as "That is not exactly what I said" or "I
was talking off the cuff".
While Genette maps the textual threshold, Pierre Bourdieu explains the socio-economic engine
driving it. In The Rules of Art and "The Production of Belief," Bourdieu analyzes the artistic field
as an "economy of symbolic goods" predicated on the collective disavowal and repression of
narrow economic interests and commercial calculation. Because the symbolic and financial
value of an artwork cannot be derived solely from the cost of its raw materials or manual labor
hours, the field requires a specialized discursive apparatus to manufacture belief in the work's
consecrated, autonomous status. Within this symbolic economy, the influential critic, biographer,
dealer, or curator does not merely comment upon a pre-existing artist; rather, by conferring
institutional consecration and symbolic capital, the meta-discursive agent acts as the "creator of
the creator" (créateur du créateur).
Finally, rhetorical theory reveals that these meta-discursive forms are inhabited by stylized
human masks—personas—that structure the power dynamics of aesthetic judgment. Rather
than treating art writing as a transparent window into an author's biography or an objective
scientific measurement, rhetorical criticism views symbolic acts as situated strategies that
constitute identity and social reality. Drawing from Wayne C. Booth's The Rhetoric of Fiction
(1961), the speaking voice within any meta-discourse functions as the First Persona or "implied
author": a rhetorical accomplishment constructed by the real author and construed by the reader
through stylistic tokens in the text rather than external biographical facts. Whether an artist
writes as a humble medieval monk or a militant avant-garde insurgent, or a critic writes as a
detached scientist or an intoxicated poet, they are performing a First Persona.
In his landmark 1970 essay "The Second Persona," Edwin Black extended Booth's framework
to address how rhetorical texts invite moral and ideological judgment. Rejecting the formulaic
"neo-Aristotelian" view of rhetoric that merely assessed immediate persuasion in a specific
situation, Black argued that every discourse implies an auditor—a Second Persona—whose
ideological world is revealed through a symptomatic reading of the stylistic tokens in the
message. In art meta-discourse, the text tells the beholder who they must become, whether an
aristocrat of taste, a sentimental citizen, or a radical comrade, to enter the moral-political
universe of the work. Pushing rhetorical criticism further into its "ideological turn," Philip Wander
(1983/1984) introduced the Third Persona: the "being in history" whose presence, though
directly relevant to what is being said, is negated through silence, exclusion, and
marginalization. Throughout art history, every dominant meta-discourse relies on a Third
Persona—from the "ultracrepidarian" manual laborer barred from judging classical painting, to
the bourgeois philistine mocked by modernism, to the uncredited human artists whose scraped
datasets train contemporary generative artificial intelligence. Synthesizing these layers, Maurice
Charland's theory of "constitutive rhetoric" demonstrates that even Black's position was not
radical enough if it accepted the pre-existence of a "person" who merely chooses to identify with
a textual persona. Instead, rhetoric is a way of being and becoming: art meta-discourse actively
constitutes the ontological categories of "artist," "artwork," "critic," and "public".

Classical Antiquity and the Medieval Scriptorium:
Craft, Mimesis, and Anagogical Vision
Classical Antiquity: Ekphrasis, the Sovereign Virtuoso, and the
Ultracrepidarian Boundary
In Classical Greece and Rome, visual art production was institutionally categorized as
techne—manual craft governed by rule-bound physical labor—while the meta-discourse
surrounding it was controlled by philosophers, rhetoricians, and encyclopedists. Plato and
Aristotle established the foundational philosophical meta-discourse by interrogating mimesis,
the hierarchy of the senses (both ranking touch well below sight), and the relationship of visual
form to the human soul. Alongside philosophical treatises, classical literary culture developed
ekphrasis, a rhetorical mode of vivid description in which the orator or poet attributed a living
voice and psychological interiority to a mute statue or painting, staging a competition between
verbal eloquence and visual illusion.
The most consequential classical meta-discourse for the long-term history of artistic personas,
however, emerged in the anecdotal historiography of Pliny the Elder's Naturalis Historia (Book
35) and Quintilian's Institutio Oratoria (XII.10.6). Centering on the fourth-century BCE painter
Apelles of Kos—who worked with a restricted four-color palette of white, red, yellow, and black
covered with a specialized translucent varnish—Pliny constructed two enduring paradigms of
aesthetic authority: the policing of critical jurisdiction and the artist as market-consecrating
connoisseur. In Book 35.85, Pliny records that Apelles habitually displayed his finished paintings
in public while hiding behind the panel to overhear the critiques of passersby. When a
shoemaker (sutor) approached and pointed out a defect in the rendering of a sandal (crepida,
from the Greek krepis), Apelles duly corrected the error. Emboldened by this success, the
shoemaker returned and began criticizing the representation of the subject's leg. Indignant,
Apelles emerged from his hiding place and issued the reprimand that immediately became
proverbial across antiquity: "Ne supra crepidam sutor iudicaret" ("Let a shoemaker not judge
beyond the shoe").
The structural implications of this anecdote for art meta-discourse are threefold. First, Apelles
executes a sharp jurisdictional boundary-work, bifurcating the artwork into a lower register of
empirical, mechanical accuracy—where the manual tradesman's domain knowledge is
valid—and a higher, synthetic register of anatomical grace and aesthetic judgment, where the
layman is strictly disenfranchised. Second, by silencing the cobbler above the sandal, classical
meta-discourse constructs the manual worker and uninitiated spectator as Wander's Third
Persona: relevant as a passive beholder, but negated as a critical voice. Third, this
boundary-work enjoyed a vast ideological afterlife. Revived by Petrarch and Quattrocento
humanists to elevate painting above guild mechanics, painted by Giorgio Vasari on the walls of
his own home in Florence (Casa Vasari), and codified in Erasmus's Adages (Ne sutor ultra
crepidam), the dictum was weaponized in 1819 by the English Romantic essayist William Hazlitt
in a ferocious open letter to Quarterly Review editor William Gifford. Hazlitt coined the term
ultracrepidarian—followed four years later in 1823 by his friend Leigh Hunt's satire
Ultra-Crepidarius: A Satire on William Gifford—to denounce pedantic critics who pass judgment
beyond their expertise. Conversely, Karl Marx ridiculed the proverb in Volume I of Capital as
outmoded guildfetishism, writing that "'Ne sutor ultra crepidam'—this nec plus ultra of handicraft
wisdom became sheer nonsense, from the moment the watchmaker Watt invented the
steam-engine, the barber Arkwright the throstle, and the working-jeweller Fulton the steamship".
While excluding the cobbler, Pliny simultaneously cast Apelles in the First Persona of the
Sovereign Courtly Virtuoso and Peer-Connoisseur. When his gifted contemporary Protogenes
was unappreciated by his own countrymen in Rhodes, Apelles sailed to visit him. Finding
Protogenes absent from his studio, Apelles painted a single, impossibly fine colored line across
a large panel; upon returning, Protogenes immediately recognized Apelles' hand via this
non-verbal peritextual signature and drew an even finer line in another color directly over the
first, prompting Apelles to return and split the lines a third time. Furthermore, Pliny notes
(XXXV.88, 81) that Apelles single-handedly established Protogenes' reputation by buying up his
rival's canvases and spreading the rumor that he intended to sell them as his own. Centuries
before the modern gallery system, Pliny's meta-discourse already operated on Bourdieu's core
mechanism: raw painterly skill remained socially invisible until consecrated by a "creator of the
creator".
### The Medieval Scriptorium and Abbey: The Exegetical Monk and the Anagogical Prelate
During the Middle Ages, classical humanism gave way to a Christian cosmological order in
which painters, metalworkers, and sculptors operated largely as anonymous artisans within
monastic workshops or urban guilds. Yet medieval art creation was accompanied by a
sophisticated dual meta-discourse that divided along institutional lines: the practical-theological
craft manual (schedula) written from within the workshop, and the liturgical-anagogical apology
written by the ecclesiastical patron.
The pinnacle of the workshop treatise is the twelfth-century De diversis artibus (On Diverse
Arts, or Schedula diversarum artium), written in the voice of Theophilus Presbyter, a
pseudonym widely associated with the Benedictine monk and master metalworker Roger of
Helmarshausen (creator of the portable altars of Paderborn and Stavelot and the Enger Cross,
active c. 1100–1125). Frequently bound in medieval manuscripts directly alongside Vitruvius's
De architectura, and drawing on didactic traditions of agriculture and medicine, Theophilus's
treatise is far more than a secular recipe book; as Heidi C. Gearhart's scholarship
demonstrates, he constructs the First Persona of the Humble Monastic Pedagogue and
Exegetical Craftsman. Across his three books and their theological prologues, Theophilus maps
the empirical transformation of earthly matter onto an eschatological trajectory structured by the
gifts of the Holy Spirit. Book One (on painting) is grounded in Humility and Fear of the Lord,
instructing the artisan to labor without ostentation. Book Two (on stained glass) invokes
Fortitude, Counsel, and Understanding, detailing the arduous physical labor required to maintain
sustainably hot furnaces fueled by copious beechwood, fashion brushes from the hair of a
badger, mule, cat, or donkey, and fix pigments onto glass using urine or wine. Book Three (on
liturgical metalwork) culminates in Wisdom and Perfection, guiding the goldsmith in crafting
chalices, censers, and crosses that manifest the triumph of the Church and build the walls of the
Heavenly Jerusalem on earth. For Theophilus, manual craft is an exegetical discipline that
sanctifies both material nature and the monastic laborer.
If Theophilus speaks as the sanctifying hand, Abbot Suger of Saint-Denis (d. 1151) speaks as
the Ecclesiastical Impresario and Anagogical Visionary. Defending his lavish architectural and
decorative reconstruction of the Royal Abbey of Saint-Denis (c. 1140–1144) by calling on glass
painters and goldsmiths from several regions, Suger composed treatises that shifted the
meta-discourse from how art is fabricated to how material splendor acts upon the beholder's
soul. Gazing upon the bejeweled altar cross of St. Eligius and the translucent "anagogical
window" in the Saint-Denis chevet, Suger describes being transported in an "anagogical
manner" (anagogicus mos)—an upward-leading spiritual ascent—two decades before Richard
of Saint-Victor articulated his formal taxonomy of mystical vision. Suger writes of inhabiting a
"strange region of the universe" (extranea orbis terrarum plaga), a liminal threshold suspended
"between the slime of the earth and the purity of heaven" (terrarum faece ... coeli puritate).
While Erwin Panofsky popularized a direct link between Suger's metaphysics of light and the
theology of Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, recent scholarship by Andreas Speer and
Stephen Murray has disproven this direct dependency, situating Suger instead within a broader
medieval way of seeing that bridges the physical eye and the "mind's eye". By framing stained
glass and gold metalwork as ontological conduits to the divine, Suger legitimized institutional
expenditure and established the Prelate-Patron as the supreme conceptual author of the sacred
environment.

The Renaissance: Humanist Treatises,
Historiographical Myth-Making, and the Alter Deus
Between the late fourteenth and mid-sixteenth centuries, the social and discursive architecture
of Western art underwent a structural revolution. In the Middle Ages, painters and sculptors
were legally and socially bound to guilds alongside manual tradesmen such as carpenters and
metalworkers, their output valued for functional utility rather than personal expression. The
Renaissance overturned this hierarchy by mobilizing three interlocking meta-discursive
forms—the transitional workshop handbook, the humanist theoretical treatise, and the collective
artist biography—which progressively transformed the artist's First Persona from a pious guild
craftsman into a learned intellectual (pictor doctus), a melancholic visionary, and ultimately a
divine autonomous genius (alter deus and il divino).

From Guild Lineage to Theoretical Demiurge: Cennini, Alberti, and
Ficino
The threshold of this transition is captured in Cennino Cennini's Il libro dell'arte (The
Craftsman's Handbook), composed around 1390 in Padua, where it absorbed Venetian dialect
terms and was dedicated to St. Anthony of Padua among other saints. Surviving in three
manuscript copies and referenced by Giorgio Vasari into the seventeenth century before its
early nineteenth-century rediscovery stimulated a tempera revival among painters such as
Birmingham artist Joseph Southall (1861–1944), Cennini's text bridges two worlds. While
preserving the medieval recipe-book structure, Cennini introduces a new genealogical
self-consciousness by tracing his artistic lineage back to Giotto, positioning the craftsman as an
heir to an evolving stylistic tradition.
Four decades later, Leon Battista Alberti severed the link to the medieval guild manual entirely
in his treatise De pictura (On Painting, 1435/1436), alongside his influential treatises on
sculpture, architecture, and literature. A quintessential humanist scholar, poet, philosopher, and
architect who designed landmark structures for the Rucellai family in Florence and Rimini,
Alberti constructed the First Persona of the Learned Theorist-Practitioner (Pictor Doctus).
Insisting that painting and sculpture required rigorous theoretical reasoning and mastery of
geometry, anatomy, philosophy, and history, Alberti campaigned to elevate the visual arts from
manual trades to the level of the liberal arts. Crucially, as early as 1436 in On Painting, Alberti
suggested that through the mathematical mastery of optical illusion and design, the artist may
well consider himself, as it were, "another god"—an alter deus.
Shortly thereafter, Florentine Neoplatonism supplied the psychological interiority for this
demiurgic persona. Through his philosophical writings, Marsilio Ficino forged a potent alliance
between Platonic "madness" (furor) and Aristotelian "melancholy," demonstrating how Plato and
Aristotle were in agreement regarding the creative soul. Previously reserved for philosophers
and poets, this "Born Under Saturn" persona was rapidly assimilated by Renaissance visual
artists from Botticelli to Michelangelo: solitary brooding, emotional volatility, and refusal to abide
by mercantile workshop routines were re-coded in the meta-discourse as essential, symptomatic
proof of transcendent creative genius.

Giorgio Vasari and the Teleological Engine of Art History
The institutionalization of these personas culminated in Giorgio Vasari's Le Vite de' più
eccellenti pittori, scultori, e architettori (Lives of the Most Excellent Painters, Sculptors, and
Architects, 1550/1568). Already a renowned painter and architect, Vasari had begun collecting
the drawings of early painters in his youth to learn through imitation, and in 1546 the humanist
historian Paolo Giovio strongly encouraged him to turn his lifelong "notes and memoranda" into
a comprehensive historical treatise. Collaborating with contemporary humanists such as the
historian Adriani and the philologist and grammarian Borghini, Vasari emerged as the founder of
the discipline of art history, casting himself in the First Persona of the Artist-Historiographer and
Connoisseur-Arbiter.
Vasari's Vite operates as a dual-layered meta-discursive machine composed of four theoretical
prefaces (proemi—a general prolegomenon on technique followed by three historical essays
preceding the biographical sections) and individual artist biographies. In the proemi, Vasari
formulates a cyclical, organic scheme of historical progression governed by the laws of nature:
the revival of true art in Tuscany from its origins in disegno through Cimabue and Giotto; its
steady maturation under Ghiberti, Brunelleschi, and Donatello; and its triumphant culmination in
Leonardo da Vinci, Raphael, and Michelangelo, whom Vasari designates as godly (divini)
because they surpassed both nature and classical antiquity. In the biographies, which he frames
as "the true mirror of human life," Vasari echoed Ficino's alliance of Platonic madness and
Aristotelian melancholy to portray artists as heroic figures driven by inner compulsion rather
than external patronage demands. His vivid myth-making anecdotes—such as Michelangelo
boldly telling Pope Julius II that the Sistine Chapel ceiling would be finished only "when it
satisfies me as an artist"—cemented the modern persona of the autonomous creator.
Remarkably, Vasari recognized that written meta-discourse could eclipse artistic execution itself.
In his biography of Leon Battista Alberti, Vasari observes that while perfection requires uniting
theory and practice in one person, Alberti's theoretical writings possessed such authority that "it
is commonly supposed that he surpassed all those who were actually his superiors in art. Thus
it is clear from experience that, with respect to fame and name, writings enjoy the greatest
power and vitality, for books easily penetrate" everywhere. At the same time, Vasari exposed the
acute socioeconomic contradiction beneath his own rhetoric of divine genius: despite the rise of
court academies and individual stylistic fame, he lamented in his introductions that if
contemporary artists were justly rewarded they would surpass the ancient world, whereas in
reality "the artist today struggles to ward off famine rather than to win fame, and this crushes
and buries his talent and obscures his name".

The Seventeenth and Eighteenth Centuries:
Quantitative Connoisseurship, Enlightenment Salons,
and Transcendental Genius
By the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the institutionalization of state academies
(most notably the French Académie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture), the expansion of an
international art market fueled by the Grand Tour, and the birth of biennial public exhibitions
fractured art meta-discourse into four distinct regimes, each generating a specialized persona.

Roger de Piles and the Quantitative Balance des Peintres
Within the French Académie Royale, orthodox meta-discourse was dominated by Poussinisme,
a dogmatic insistence that rectilinear drawing (disegno) and classical contour constituted the
rational foundation of art, while color was merely a secondary, sensual ornament. Challenging
the establishment of his time—at a historical moment when intellectual nonconformity in France
carried severe risks—the theorist and critic Roger de Piles championed Rubénisme (modeled
on Peter Paul Rubens), declaring in his Dialogue sur le coloris (1673) that "there is no painting if
color does not go with drawing" and that "color is the soul of painting".
In 1708, De Piles published the most audacious meta-discursive experiment of the early modern
era: the Balance des peintres (The Balance of Painters). Adopting the First Persona of the
Taxonomic Quantifier and Playful Arbiter, De Piles decomposed artistic value into four
fundamental characteristics—Composition, Drawing, Color, and Expression—and graded 56
canonical painters from his own and previous eras on a numerical scale from 0 to 20 for each
attribute. Although De Piles claimed defensively that the Balance "was written for my own
pleasure, rather than to attract others to my view," his contemporaries took it very seriously as a
"clever way to characterize genius," while later traditional art historians such as Julius von
Schlosser despised its quantitative reductionism.

A close inspection of De Piles' matrix reveals how quantitative meta-discourse operates as an
ideological weapon. Although De Piles was careful not to explicitly sum the four grades himself,
an unweighted summation yields a total score of 50 for Rembrandt (15 in Composition, 6 in
Drawing, 17 in Color, and 12 in Expression) compared to only 37 for academic idol
Michelangelo—a radical inversion of Renaissance disegno orthodoxy that persists even if
Drawing is weighted three times as heavily as the other attributes (\beta_2 = 3, yielding 62 for
Rembrandt). Simultaneously, De Piles' personal biases surface in his disparagement of early
Venetian expression: while awarding Leonardo da Vinci 14 points in Expression, he assigned
Giovanni Bellini a zero in Expression, alongside 4 for Giorgione and 6 for Titian.
Remarkably, econometric research by Victor Ginsburgh and Sheila Weyers demonstrates that
De Piles' Balance anticipated the philosophical "test of time" far better than the orthodox
Académie under André Félibien. While the space De Piles devoted to individual artists in his
own biographical Abrégé correlated only mildly with his Balance scores (yielding 7.4 additional
lines per unit increase in score, with all four characteristics carrying roughly equal weight), his
Color ratings strongly predict canonical survival and market valuation centuries later. Each
additional point De Piles awarded in Color corresponds to an increase of 64.6 lines in Turner's
Dictionary of Art and an 11.9% premium in modern auction prices, whereas his ratings for
Drawing carry a statistical coefficient not significantly different from zero. By codifying Color as
an independent, quantifiable metric, De Piles aligned critical discourse with the tastes of Louis
XIV's court and prefigured the sensory turn of modern art markets.

Jonathan Richardson and the Empirical "Science of a Connoisseur"
Eleven years after De Piles' Balance, the English portraitist and writer Jonathan Richardson
(Senior, later joined by his son Jonathan Richardson Junior, who corrected and prepared his
father's works for the press) became the first person to develop a systematic method of art
criticism in English with his Two Discourses: I. An Essay on the Whole Art of Criticism as It
Relates to Painting; II. An Argument in Behalf of the Science of a Connoisseur (1719),
followed by their An Account of Some of the Statues, Bas-reliefs, Drawings and Pictures in Italy,
etc. with Remarks (1722).
Addressing the rising British gentry and merchant elite embarking on the Grand Tour,
Richardson constructed the First Persona of the Rational Gentleman-Connoisseur and
projected a corresponding Second Persona: the empirically trained, civic-minded collector.
Expressly avoiding mere mechanical cataloging, Richardson argued that attribution and quality
assessment constituted a methodical "science" possessing "dignity, certainty, pleasure, and
advantage". Crucially, Richardson's analytical procedure depended upon maintaining a strict
cognitive barrier between the spectator and the painting—a cool, judicial detachment that
nineteenth-century Romantic critics like John Ruskin would later abandon in their defense of
J.M.W. Turner and the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood.

Denis Diderot and the Embodied, Polyphonic Salon
If Richardson erected a judicial barrier between critic and canvas, Denis Diderot demolished it.
Beginning in 1759 and continuing until 1781, Diderot reviewed the biennial exhibitions of the
Paris Académie (the Salons) for his friend Friedrich Melchior, Baron von Grimm's manuscript
newsletter, the Correspondance littéraire. Having spent 27 years building the 72,000-article
Encyclopédie—where he personally visited workshops to document manual trades and used
subversive cross-references (such as sending readers from the polite article "Cordelier" to
"Capuchon" to mock monks, or hiding a defense of free speech under the Roman god "Aius
Locutius") to evade royal censors—Diderot brought a materialist, sensory, and dialogic
sensibility to art criticism.
Realizing that "the looser the rules, the more relative the standards by which art could be
judged," Diderot recognized that unconventional art required an unconventional criticism to give
it credibility and a raison d'être. In his Salons (especially from the pivotal 1765 installment
onward), Diderot invented the First Persona of the Embodied, Polyphonic Philosophe and
Sentimental Dramatist. As Jean Starobinski noted, Diderot was a creator of literary hybrids who
favored satire and dialogue to welcome heterogeneity. In his Salons, Diderot relishes dispute:
he argues for the renewal of art as a moral authority, quarrels with artists while lending them
audible voices within his text that stand in contrast to his own opinion, banters directly with
Grimm ("Can you see how beautiful she is! How interesting she is! I hate to trouble her"), and
continually argues with himself.
Furthermore, Diderot replaces analytical detachment with diegetic immersion, staging himself as
an embodied viewer in active sensory and cognitive exchange with the canvas. In his celebrated
analysis of Jean-Baptiste Greuze's Girl Weeping Over Her Dead Bird (1765), Diderot becomes
an "intoxicated critic" who steps across the frame to introduce an entire elegy of his own
invention. First marveling at the painterly execution ("Oh! what a lovely hand, cries the
intoxicated critic as he gazes at her; what a lovely hand! what lovely arms! Observe the
accuracy of the detail of those fingers, and those dimples, and that soft flesh, and the reddish
tint of the finger tips caused by the pressure of the head"), Diderot then draws near and speaks
directly to the painted figure to lift her veil of mystery: "Why, my dear, your grief is very great,
very profound. What means this dreamy, melancholy expression? What! all for a bird? You are
not weeping, you are in deep affliction; and your affliction is accompanied by thought. Come, my
dear, open your heart to me; tell me the truth: is it really this bird's death that has withdrawn you
so entirely and so sadly into yourself?". By transfixing the visual idyll with his own narrative
elegy—while later arguing in his theatrical dialogue Paradoxe sur le comédien (1770s/1830) that
a great performer must rely on cool technical artifice rather than raw emotional
identification—Diderot transformed art criticism into an autonomous literary performance that
directly prefigured Marcel Proust's narrator contemplating the paintings of Elstir in À la
recherche du temps perdu.

Immanuel Kant and the Transcendental Oracle of Nature
At the close of the eighteenth century, Immanuel Kant's Critique of Judgment (Kritik der
Urteilskraft, 1790, §§ 43–50) synthesized Enlightenment aesthetics and laid the philosophical
cornerstone for Romantic and Modernist meta-discourse. In § 43, Kant established a rigorous
three-part demarcation of art in general. First, art is distinguished from nature as rational making
through freedom (facere), which produces a work (opus), whereas nature operates through
mechanical or instinctual acting (agere), which yields an effect (effectus, such as bees
constructing honeycomb cells). Second, art as practical human skill is distinguished from
science as theoretical knowledge; possessing complete theoretical knowledge does not
automatically confer the practical skill to make an object, just as Petrus Camper could describe
with scientific exactitude how the best shoe must be made without being able to fashion a shoe
himself. Third, "free" (fine) art is distinguished from mercenary industrial handicraft: free art
succeeds only as play—an occupation agreeable on its own account—whereas handicraft is
labor or drudgery attractive only for its external pay, though Kant cautions against newer
proto-Romantic leaders who believe free art is promoted by sweeping away all compulsory
"mechanism", without which the free spirit of art becomes "bodyless and evanescent".
Having isolated fine art, Kant confronted a profound philosophical problem: pure judgments of
taste rest on appreciating an object's "purposiveness without a purpose" (devoid of any
determinate concept), yet every artwork is intentionally produced. To resolve this tension, Kant
codified the persona of the Innate Rule-Giving Genius in § 46:
"Genius is the talent (natural endowment) which gives the rule to art. Since talent, as an innate
productive faculty of the artist, belongs itself to nature, we may express the matter thus: Genius
is the innate mental disposition (ingenium) through which nature gives the rule to art."
Through this transcendental formulation, Kant fundamentally restructured the relationship
between the artist and meta-discourse. Because beautiful art cannot be derived from
determinate rules or concepts, genius is a talent for producing that for which no determinate rule
can be given; its primary property is exemplary originality, creating models that establish schools
for other "good minds" while ignoring classical rules. Crucially, Kant stipulates that "Genius itself
cannot describe or indicate scientifically how it brings about its products, and it is rather nature
[through the talent of the artist] that gives the rule to art", prescribing rules not to science but
exclusively to fine art. Because it is "nature in the subject" operating from pre-conceptual,
noumenal intuition rather than conscious procedural design, the artist-genius is philosophically
absolved—and barred—from explaining their own work. This Kantian persona of the mute,
nature-favored oracle dominated nineteenth-century Romanticism, until Friedrich Nietzsche
subjected it to a piercing sociological critique: Nietzsche argued that the cult of innate,
unlearnable genius is a self-serving myth embraced by the audience (the Second Persona)
because it functions to allow ordinary people "to be content with a life of mediocrity and soothed
in our inability to work toward some lofty objective," attributing great works to magical natural
gifts rather than relentless discipline, perseverance, and mastery.

The Nineteenth Century: Modernity, the Flâneur, and
the Critic as Autonomous Creator
In the nineteenth century, the rise of industrial capitalism, metropolitan crowds, and mass print
journalism shattered the homogeneity of the aristocratic patron class. Facing an expanding
bourgeois public and an entrenched academic Salon system, art meta-discourse shifted from
Enlightenment consensus-building to partisan ideological warfare and aestheticist self-assertion.

Charles Baudelaire: Partisan Criticism and the Triad of Modern
Personas
Writing in his Salon de 1846 (in the programmatic opening section "À quoi bon la critique?"), the
poet-critic Charles Baudelaire issued the foundational manifesto for modern partisan criticism:
"Pour être juste, c'est-à-dire pour avoir sa raison d'être, la critique doit être partiale, passionnée,
politique, c'est-à-dire faite à un point de vue exclusif, mais au point de vue qui ouvre le plus
d'horizons." ("To be just, that is to say, to justify its existence, criticism must be partial,
passionate, and political, that is to say, written from an exclusive point of view, but from a point
of view that opens up the widest horizons.")
Scorning the "reasonable and sublime" centrist critics who pedantically advised colorists to
study drawing and draughtsmen to study color—and insisting that technical studio procedures
are learned in the atelier while the public cares only for the result—Baudelaire championed an
imaginative criticism that translates the aesthetic force of artists like Eugène Delacroix or Edgar
Allan Poe into a poetic equivalent.
In his seminal essay The Painter of Modern Life (Le Peintre de la vie moderne, written
1859–1860 and published in 1863), Baudelaire reformulated both the ontology of art and the
persona of its creator-observer. Rejecting the academic dogma of a unique and absolute
classical beauty—noting that a contemporary painter who depicts a modern courtesan by taking
"inspiration" from Titian or Raphael will produce only a "false, ambiguous and obscure"
work—Baudelaire established a rational and historical theory of dual beauty. In the most famous
sentence of the essay, he declared: "Modernity is the transitory, the fleeting [le fugitif], the
contingent, half of art [la moitié de l'art], the other half of which is the eternal and the
unchangeable".
To capture this fugitive modernity, Baudelaire centered his essay not on a consecrated
academic master, but on the rapid, on-the-spot sketches of contemporary manners, fashion,
and war produced for mass-circulation periodicals like the Illustrated London News by
Constantin Guys, whom Baudelaire masks under the cipher "Monsieur G.". As critical
scholarship observes, Guys' deliberate anonymity—what Baudelaire calls the "fiction" of his
subject's "incognito"—allowed Baudelaire to use art criticism for his own ends, constructing a
composite Triad of Modern Personas that merged the artist and the critic into a single protean
consciousness. First, Monsieur G. is the Flâneur and Man of Crowds: a passionate spectator for
whom the urban crowd is his natural element, just as air is to birds and water to fishes. Rejoicing
in his incognito like a disguised prince, the flâneur sets up house in the heart of the multitude
amid the ebb and flow of movement, acting as "a mirror as vast as the crowd itself" and "a
kaleidoscope gifted with consciousness," an "I with an insatiable appetite for the 'non-I'" who
distills the eternal from the transitory. Second, he is the Man-Child or Convalescent: recently
returned from the shadow of death, he breathes in all the odors and essences of life with
rapture, driven by a curiosity that has become a fatal, irresistible passion and possessing "the
genius of childhood—a genius for which no aspect of life has become stale". Third, he intersects
with the Dandy, who possesses a quintessence of character and a subtle understanding of the
moral mechanism of the world, yet aspires to aristocratic insensitivity and self-mastery in
opposition to bourgeois vulgarity.

Oscar Wilde and the Sovereign Critic-as-Artist
If Baudelaire elevated the poet-critic to parity with the painter, Oscar Wilde inverted the
hierarchy entirely, crowning the critic as the supreme creative artist. In his two-part Platonic
dialogue "The Critic as Artist: With Some Remarks on the Importance of Doing Nothing
and Discussing Everything" (originally published in the July and September 1890 issues of
The Nineteenth Century as "The True Function and Value of Criticism," and substantially revised
in his 1891 collection Intentions), Wilde staged a philosophical conversation between two
Aestheticist personas: the leading voice Gilbert and his interlocutor Ernest.
Speaking through Gilbert—performing the First Persona of the Refined Aesthete and
Paradoxical Dandy-Critic—Wilde systematically demolished the boundary between art and
criticism. Against the Romantic and Kantian notion of the artist creating from an unconscious
outpouring of emotion, Gilbert posits that all fine imaginative work is self-conscious and
deliberate: "No poet sings because he must. At least, no great poet does. A great poet sings
because he chooses to sing... There is no fine art without self-consciousness, and
self-consciousness and the critical spirit are one". While artistic creation is entirely dependent
upon the working of the critical faculty, criticism itself is both creative and wholly independent of
the object it criticizes. Just as the work of a poet or sculptor is not judged by low standards of
imitation or resemblance, criticism is "never trammelled by any shackles of verisimilitude" or
ignoble considerations of probability. To the critic, the primary artwork—even the dull poems of
Lewis Morris, the novels of Georges Ohnet, or the plays of Henry Arthur Jones—serves merely
as raw material or a starting suggestion out of which the critic fashions "a creation within a
creation" that is flawless in beauty and intellectual subtlety. When Ernest questions this
autonomy, Gilbert delivers the ultimate Aestheticist credo: "One may appeal from fiction unto
fact. But from the soul there is no appeal... That is what the highest criticism really is, the record
of one's own soul. It is more fascinating than history, as it is concerned simply with oneself. It is
more delightful than philosophy, as its subject is concrete and not abstract, real and not vague".
With Wilde, the nineteenth-century epitext achieved complete ontological sovereignty over the
artwork.

The Twentieth Century: Avant-Garde Manifestos,
Formalist Tribunals, Conceptualism, and the
Curatorial Turn
Across the twentieth century, the struggle over aesthetic legitimation intensified into a series of
institutional coups. Artists, critics, and curators competed to control the paratextual threshold,
progressively dematerializing the art object until the meta-discourse absorbed the artwork
altogether.

The Avant-Garde Manifesto and the Militant Insurgent
Between 1885 and 1915, spanning European modernism's most turbulent decades, the artist
manifesto emerged as the primary epitextual weapon for legitimizing radical artistic practice.
Confronting profound social and cultural transformations that alienated the artist from bourgeois
norms, modernist practitioners used manifesto writing to strip off the robes of office and the
compliant social persona assigned to them by the middle class. In their place, the manifesto
constructed the First Persona of the Militant Insurgent and Revolutionary Vanguard. Adopting a
binary, combative mode of argument that mocked established artistic conventions whose time
had passed, the manifesto institutionalized an explicitly antagonistic relationship between the
artist and the bourgeois public (relegated to the Third Persona), while deploying theoretical
discourse preemptively to dictate how the visual work had to be read.

Mid-Century Critical Hegemony: Greenberg's Tribunal vs.
Rosenberg's Arena
As the center of the Western avant-garde shifted to America in the mid-twentieth century, the
radical abstraction of Abstract Expressionism prompted artists to write criticism defending their
work and elevated professional New York art critics to unprecedented institutional power.
Mid-century meta-discourse was defined by the ideological clash between Clement Greenberg
and Harold Rosenberg (alongside Lawrence Alloway, who introduced "other criteria" grounded
in mass culture and information systems). Greenberg assumed the First Persona of the Stern
Formalist Arbiter, evaluating the finished artwork as an autonomous, medium-specific physical
object governed by flatness and optical purity. Conversely, Rosenberg forged the persona of the
Existential Witness, famously defining "Action Painting" not as the production of a static object,
but as a dramatic performance, gesture, and existential event in which the canvas serves as an
arena in which to act.

Conceptual Art and the Liquidation of the Critic: Joseph Kosuth
By the late 1960s, a new generation of artists recognized that Greenbergian formalism had
reduced the practitioner to a mute producer of optical objects awaiting the critic's consecration.
Reaching back to Marcel Duchamp's 1917 readymade Fountain—which demonstrated that an
artist could create art merely by nominating an industrial object, signing it with a peritextual
pseudonym, and placing it within an institutional context—American artist Joseph Kosuth
became the father of articulated Conceptualism.
In 1965, Kosuth created his foundational piece One and Three Chairs (a structure he
subsequently reprised with a shovel, a mirror, a hammer, and a saw), presenting a chair as one
concept across three entities: a physical chair, a photograph of that chair installed in the room,
and an enlarged photostat of a dictionary definition of the word "chair". Crucially, every time the
work is exhibited in a new location, the physical chair and its accompanying photograph change,
while only the dictionary definition remains unchanged. Four years later, in his landmark
1969 essay "Art after Philosophy" (collected in Art after Philosophy and After: Collected
Writings, 1966–1990), Kosuth articulated the theoretical framework that One and Three Chairs
materialized. By defining Conceptual Art as an analytic proposition in which the linguistic and
philosophical inquiry into the nature of art is the artwork itself, Kosuth assumed the First
Persona of the Artist-Philosopher and Analytic Linguist, effectively liquidating the role of the
traditional formalist critic.

The Curatorial Turn and the Créateur du Créateur
Although Conceptual Art bypassed the formalist critic, the late-twentieth-century art world
quickly generated a new institutional sovereign: the Independent Curator. As theorized by Paul
O'Neill in "The Curatorial Turn: From Practice to Discourse" (and explored by Hal Foster
and Hans Ulrich Obrist), the late 1980s and 1990s witnessed a structural shift in which
individual moments of exhibition practice translated into a hierarchical "common discourse" of
curating, elevating curating into a distinct "medium of artistic practice". Through international
biennials and thematic exhibitions, the curator assumed the First Persona of the Meta-Artist and
Discursive Auteur, acting as Bourdieu's supreme "creator of the creator"—even as O'Neill warns
that, instead of preserving curating as one of the rare intellectual positions in the processes of
art circulation, market pressures threaten to reduce curators to mere administrative brokers.

The Contemporary Era: Video Essays, Algorithmic
Process Labor, and Post-Human AI Discourse
In the first quarter of the twenty-first century, the migration of cultural reception onto algorithmic
digital platforms and the emergence of generative artificial intelligence have transfigured media
content, discourse, and criticism through an internet-shaped prism. Today, art meta-discourse is
increasingly governed by three contemporary formations: the YouTube video essay, the
short-form process video on TikTok and Instagram Reels, and the post-human
prompt-and-dataset discourse of generative AI.

The YouTube Video Essay and the Parasocial Public Intellectual
Finding its home on YouTube in the early 2010s through pioneering channels such as Every
Frame a Painting, The Nerdwriter (created by former MSNBC producer Evan Puschak),
Wisecrack, Screen Junkies, and Solar Sands, the video essay emerged as a distinct
multimodal discursive medium. Rather than a recording of a student reading a term paper aloud,
the video essay blends weeks of complex image editing, audio scoring, and written narration to
promote creative expression and deep inquiry.
Within this digital ecosystem, creators have forged distinct First Personas to mediate art and
culture for millions of subscribers. Commanding 3.15 million subscribers with a series that "puts
ideas to work," Evan Puschak (The Nerdwriter) adopts the persona of the Passionate,
Polymathic Inquirer. Explicitly rejecting academic dogmatism—stating that "The essay is not a
treatise. It's not a term paper. It's not something systematically covering everything about a
subject. It is an inquiry"—Puschak frames his videos on Shakespeare, Tarkovsky, and visual art
as a personal way of working out what he believes. In his nine-minute video essay "Leonardo da
Vinci's Best Painting (Is Not The Mona Lisa)," Puschak analyzes composition, form, and
technique to argue that Leonardo's The Virgin and Child with St. Anne (1501–1519) surpasses
the Mona Lisa by blending personal mastery of mathematics and science with spiritual
representation.
Alongside Puschak, creators like Solar Sands adopt the persona of the Archival Digital
Investigator, lending narrative gravitas to niche art-historical inquiries such as 22-minute
explorations of the "weird" titles of paintings or "The World's Most 'Wanted' Paintings" (631,000
views)—though Reddit discussions on r/MauLer and r/youtubedrama reveal how quickly digital
audiences turn on video essayists when they venture into surface-level pop-culture critiques
beyond their expertise or breach parasocial boundaries in their comment sections. Meanwhile,
emerging smaller creators like Art Chad (10.1K subscribers) deploy a fast-paced, comedic
Gen-Z Ironist persona ("videos about art, philosophy, and [Gen-Z] culture as an attempt to find
common ground before the world ends"), arguing in "3 Reasons Why Public Art is so Mid" that
contemporary civic commissions—such as the January 2023 Boston monument The Embrace
commemorating Martin Luther King Jr. and Coretta Scott King—fail because they are borne out
of municipal economic necessity, designed by insular art experts detached from the public, and
devoid of authenticity because "making art is an inherently selfish act". As critical analysis by
Noah Solomons (March 2026) observes, while video essays have made art criticism far more
accessible and creative, the structural mechanics of the attention economy increasingly push
the medium toward formulaic oversaturation and iterative excess.

Short-Form Process Videos: The Greenbergian Grid vs. the
Rosenbergian Reel
Parallel to the critical video essay, practicing visual artists on social media must navigate a
bifurcated platform architecture that directly resurrects the mid-twentieth-century debate
between Clement Greenberg and Harold Rosenberg. As contemporary art criticism notes, the
static, grid-based layout of classic Instagram is an inherently Greenbergian platform: it
forces a formalist judgment of the final object, reduced to a glossy, finished JPEG. By contrast,
TikTok and Instagram Reels are Rosenbergian platforms: dominated by short-form "process
videos" and "art reveals" that film the artist's hand beside the canvas, they celebrate the
performance, the gesture, and the temporal event of art-making.
Yet a closer inspection of digital artist training curricula—such as Maria Lia Malandrino's
Skillshare course Reels and TikTok for Digital Artists: Learn how to Boost your Art—reveals that
this digital Rosenbergianism is a highly calculated simulation of process. To maximize
algorithmic reach across Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube Shorts, artists are taught to follow a
seven-step workflow that begins not with visual inspiration, but with scouting trending audio
tracks from fashion, beauty, travel, and lifestyle communities, matching visual cuts to the
specific beats, melody changes, and voiceover pauses of the sound. Most tellingly, rather than
filming genuine, unpredictable creative struggle in real time, Malandrino advises artists to finish
the artwork first and "backtrack" their steps during the shoot: "You can either create an artwork
as you're shooting: Or use a finished one and 'backtrack' your steps during the shot: I prefer the
second option: you have more control and are more relaxed during the shot. Also, you can
shoot less footage because you're only shooting for the correct amount of time that you need".
Hitting record for only a few seconds at each stage while changing camera angles, settings, and
even clothing or accessories between takes to keep the reel visually varied, the contemporary
digital artist inhabits the First Persona of the Aesthetic Laborer and Performative
Process-Influencer, often projecting a romantic "outsider" persona while staging creation in
reverse for the algorithm.
Generative AI and the Post-Human Object-Object Relation
The most radical rupture in the history of art meta-discourse is the rise of generative artificial
intelligence, including diffusion models like Stable Diffusion (explored in collaborative human-AI
projects such as "A girl who talks to AI") and temporal video generation models trained on vast
datasets of movies and YouTube videos. Operating under the computational governing logic of
"Garbage In, Garbage Out" (GIGO), generative AI exposes profound tensions surrounding
creativity, authorship, and labor. As Dave Beech and Jens Schröter (2024) state in "A
Conversation on Art and Labour," AI replaces the historical "subject-object relation of artistic
production with an object-object relation". Simultaneously, the critical judgment of art is
undergoing automation: as Joanna Zylinska documents in "AI Art: Machine Visions and Warped
Dreams" (2020), Huawei's photography competition was adjudicated by two judges—human
photographer Alex Lambrechts and a Huawei P20 Pro smartphone equipped with a "Master AI"
programmed to evaluate thousands of images and determine optimum camera zoom and
composition. As HMVC Gallery director Vincent Chang observes, AI systematically automates
whichever creative processes human culture no longer chooses to value as human input.
When AI-generated pieces such as Théâtre D'opéra Spatial began winning human art
competitions, practitioners turned to adversarial meta-discourse to expose the crisis. German
artist Boris Eldagsen submitted his AI-generated image The Electrician to the Sony World
Photography Awards; when the competition judges awarded it first prize—admitting they could
not tell the extent of AI interaction in the creation of the photograph—Eldagsen publicly forfeited
the prize in protest to demand active institutional measures protecting human creativity. In this
post-human regime, the human operator shifts into the First Persona of the Prompt Engineer or
Adversarial Provocateur, while the millions of uncredited human artists whose scraped works
populate AI training datasets are consigned to Wander's Third Persona—structurally essential to
the machine's output, yet negated and silenced within the object-object circuit.

Synthesis and Structural Conclusions
Tracing the trajectory of art meta-discourse from classical antiquity to the contemporary
algorithmic age reveals three overarching structural laws that govern how culture produces,
legitimizes, and consumes the category of "art."

The Inverse Law of Materiality and Meta-Discourse
Across the past two and a half millennia, the volume, velocity, and ontological authority of art
meta-discourse have expanded in inverse proportion to the physical craft requirements of the art
object itself. When artistic production was anchored in arduous material transformation—such
as Theophilus Presbyter's twelfth-century beechwood furnaces and metalwork or Cennino
Cennini's Quattrocento tempera panels—meta-discourse remained largely technical,
pedagogical, and exegetical. However, as the artwork progressively dematerialized across the
modern and contemporary eras—moving from Impressionist oil sketches to Duchamp's
readymade urinal, Joseph Kosuth's photostated dictionary definitions, and contemporary text
prompts fed into diffusion models—the surrounding epitext expanded to bear the entire burden
of Pierre Bourdieu's "production of belief". When anyone can purchase a chair or type a prompt,
it is exclusively the meta-discourse that consecrates the act as art.
The Dialectical Migration of the "Creator of the Creator"
Sovereign authority within Genette's paratextual threshold has never remained static; rather, it
has migrated dialectically between practitioners and external mediators, continually redefining
who occupies the role of Bourdieu's "creator of the creator". In the Renaissance, Alberti and
Vasari wrested discursive authority away from medieval ecclesiastical patrons (such as Abbot
Suger) and guild regulations, using theoretical treatises and heroic biographies to constitute the
artist as an autonomous intellectual and divine genius. In the eighteenth and nineteenth
centuries, the rise of public Salons and print journalism transferred consecrating power to the
Connoisseur (Richardson), the Quantitative Arbiter (De Piles), the Embodied Philosophe
(Diderot), the Flâneur Poet-Critic (Baudelaire), and ultimately Wilde's Critic-as-Artist, who
reduced the primary artwork to mere raw material for the critic's soul. In the twentieth century,
avant-garde manifesto writers and Conceptualists like Kosuth liquidated the formalist critic's
tribunal by absorbing philosophy directly into the artwork, only to be subsumed in turn by the
late-twentieth-century Independent Curator, who transformed the exhibition itself into a
meta-artistic medium.

The Recursive Collapse of Personas in the Algorithmic Present
Finally, the transition to the contemporary digital and AI ecosystem has fundamentally altered
Maurice Charland's constitutive rhetoric and the relationship among the First, Second, and Third
Personas. In classical, Enlightenment, and modernist discourse, the First Persona (whether
Apelles, Diderot, or Baudelaire) projected a distinct stylistic voice outward to educate, seduce,
or antagonize the Second Persona (the implied audience). In the twenty-first-century attention
economy, this directional vector has reversed into a closed cybernetic feedback loop. When a
digital artist on TikTok or Instagram Reels selects a trending audio track first and "backtracks"
an already finished painting to simulate a Rosenbergian process event, or when a YouTube
video essayist calibrates an inquiry to platform retention metrics, the algorithmic Second
Persona actively scripts the First Persona. Simultaneously, the rise of generative AI and
automated aesthetic evaluation ("Master AI" judges) threatens to bypass the human First and
Second Personas altogether—replacing the human subject-object relation with a post-human
object-object circuit while relegating living human creators to the structural silence of the Third
Persona. Consequently, the central struggle of contemporary art discourse is no longer how to
judge the beauty of an object, but how to defend the ontological necessity of human
intentionality within the threshold of the paratext.
