# Commissioning Package & Editorial Guide
## The Eight Primary Critical Discourses in Literary Theory

**Document type:** Editorial commissioning package and style governance guide
**Version:** 2.0 (realigned to The Category System v1.1)
**Prepared:** November 2026; revised November 2026
**Scope:** Commissioning, prompting, templating, and quality-controlling critical review deliverables written through eight preset critical discourses — formalist, biographical, historical, psychoanalytic, reader-response, Marxist, feminist, and deconstructionary criticism — each a coordinate region in the category system, with hybrid regions commissionable on request.

---

# Part I — Editorial Framework

## 1.1 Purpose and Scope

This package equips an editorial desk to commission, prompt, template, and quality-control critical reviews of literary works, where each review is written through exactly one of the eight primary critical discourses. It contains, for each discourse:

1. **A writer profile** — the orientation of critic to recruit, stated in coordinates (pole, mode, posture) with voice, signature moves, and hiring red flags; the profile describes orientation and does not determine format
2. **Required theoretical background** — the canonical thinkers, key texts, core concepts, and method toolkit the writer must command, plus evidence-of-competence checks for hiring.
3. **Exact prompt specifications** — literal, copy-paste commissioning prompt text with variables, ready to issue.
4. **A structured output template** — the section-by-section skeleton, with word budgets, that every deliverable in that discourse must follow.

The package assumes the standard deliverable is a **critical review of a single literary work (a novel, play, poetry collection, or story collection) through a single critical lens**, written for an educated general readership at scholarly rigor.

## 1.2 How to Use This Package

**Commissioning workflow:**

1. **Select the discourse(s).** Match the work under review to the discourse(s) that will generate the strongest critical yield. A single work may be commissioned across multiple discourses as separate deliverables — but never as a blend.
2. **Recruit against the writer profile.** Use the profile in each section as the hiring specification and voice-brief. Profiles describe a writer’s orientation (pole, mode, posture); format is assigned independently.
3. **Issue the prompt spec verbatim.** Copy the prompt block from the relevant section, fill the variables (Appendix A), and send. Do not paraphrase the prompt; the constraints are calibrated to the discourse's known failure modes.
4. **Enforce the output template.** Reject at triage any submission that does not follow the discourse template's section order and word budgets.
5. **Run the QA rubric** (§1.4) plus the discourse-specific checks (Appendix C).
6. **Route through the revision protocol** (§3.2) as needed.

**The region rule.** Every commission specifies a coordinate region in the category system (pole, axis, modes — see *The Category System*). Pure regions are the default; hybrid regions (two axes, a pole pair) are first-class commissions. Drift means leaving the commissioned region — a Marxist review that slides into uncommissioned psychoanalytic claims — and is a structural defect at QA, checked against the commission’s own coordinate block, not against a fixed shelf.

## 1.3 Universal Deliverable Standards

All eight discourses share these baseline specifications:

| Standard | Specification |
|---|---|
| Length | 2,200 words ±10% of body text (Works Cited excluded) |
| Citation style | MLA, 9th edition |
| Primary text | Directly quoted, with act/scene/line, chapter, or page references; minimum 8 quotations |
| Secondary sources | Minimum 3 scholarly sources from the discourse's required canon or peer-reviewed extensions of it |
| Plot summary | No more than 150 words, and only in service of analysis (enforced in every prompt spec) |
| Register | Educated general reader; scholarly precision without jargon walls; every technical term defined at first use |
| Person and tense | First person permitted where the discourse's method requires it (reader-response, some feminist and deconstructive modes); otherwise third person, present tense for textual events |
| Word budgets | Section budgets in Part II templates are targets; distribute any remainder proportionally across the analytical sections so the body total meets the length standard |
| Plagiarism | Zero tolerance; verified at intake; quotation and paraphrase conventions enforced line by line |
| AI-use policy | Disclosed per house policy; the writer remains responsible for every claim, quotation, and citation |

## 1.4 Cross-Discourse QA Rubric

Every submission is scored on six dimensions. A submission must score **4 or higher on every dimension** to pass; any dimension below 3 is an automatic return to the writer.

One scoring addition: every template’s deliverable evaluates, so the verdict must **name and operationalize its standard** — the criterion of worth, with what would count as meeting it. An unnamed or unoperationalized standard is scored under theoretical fidelity.

| Dimension | Weight | 5 — Excellent | 3 — Acceptable | 1 — Fail |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Theoretical fidelity | 25% | The discourse's concepts are applied accurately, generatively, and to the text's specific features | Concepts applied correctly but mechanically | Conceptual errors, name-dropping without application, or method drift |
| Textual evidence | 20% | Every major claim anchored in quoted or precisely cited textual detail | Most claims anchored; some assertion without evidence | Assertion-driven; quotes decorative or absent |
| Argumentative integrity | 20% | A sustained, surprising thesis that the review proves and that a rival reading would have to answer | Clear thesis, predictable execution | No thesis; summary or appreciation disguised as argument |
| Structural compliance | 15% | Template followed exactly; word budgets within tolerance; all required sections present | Template followed with minor deviations | Template ignored or restructured |
| Prose quality | 10% | Clean, alive, precise; register maintained throughout | Competent, occasional flatness or clutter | Unclear, bloated, or jargon-bound |
| Citation integrity | 10% | Every quotation accurate and located; secondary sources correctly attributed and actually consulted | Minor formatting errors | Misquotation, fabricated sources, or unverifiable citations — automatic rejection |

## 1.5 Common Failure Modes and Remedies

| Failure mode | Typical symptom | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Theory-dumping | Canonical names and terms deployed without application to the text | Return: require each named concept to be tied to a quoted passage |
| Summary inflation | Plot retelling crowding out analysis | Return: cut summary to ≤10%; reassign the freed budget to the template's analytical sections |
| Region drift | The review's engine leaves the commissioned coordinate region | Return as structural failure against the commission's coordinate block; reissue the block and prompt spec verbatim |
| Presentism / anachronism | Historical or feminist reviews judging the text by contemporary categories without mediation | Return: require explicit mediation of the gap between the text's moment and ours |
| Escapism verdicts | Reviews that end in nihilism (deconstruction), diagnosis of the author (psychoanalytic), or gossip (biographical) | Return: point to the discourse's verdict-section specification |
| Paraphrase dependence | Formalist reviews restating what the text "says" rather than how it is made | Return: require device-level quotation and analysis |
| Wallpaper context | Historical reviews stacking background without argument | Return: require each contextual fact to do argumentative work |
| Solipsism | Reader-response reviews reporting feelings without textual cues | Return: require the cue–response–evidence chain specified in the template |

# Part II — The Eight Discourse Commissions

The eight discourses are **presets**: fixed coordinate blocks in the category system (pole, mode, axis), with output templates generated by the generation rule — sections from aims, content from pole and mode. A desk may commission outside the eight by writing a coordinate block directly (see *The Category System*, §5.1). The eight survive because they are the field’s tested combinations, not because the shelf is closed.

## 2.1 Formalist Criticism

### 2.1.1 Writer Profile: The Formalist Critic (work pole, manifest mode)

**Hire profile.** A poet-critic or ex-poet with an MFA or MA and deep training in prosody, rhetoric, and textual analysis. Formed in the New Critical workshop tradition: able to sit with a single stanza for an hour and return with something no one has noticed. Likely publishes poetry or short fiction alongside criticism.

**Voice on the page.** Exacting, unhurried, quietly confident. Sentences build toward the quotation and then spend time inside it. Treats the literary work as a self-sufficient verbal object — an artifact whose meaning is made by its formal choices, not by its author's life, its reader's reactions, or its historical moment.

**Signature moves.**
- Opens on a small formal detail (a repeated vowel, a broken meter, a curiously placed modifier) and expands outward to a claim about the whole work's design.
- Tracks tension, irony, paradox, and ambiguity to their resolution — or productive non-resolution — in an achieved organic unity.
- Uses the phrase "the poem does" or "the novel does," never "the author wants."

**Hiring red flags.** Reaches for biography within the first page. Paraphrases where quotation is required. Describes what a passage means without ever showing how it means. Uses "beautiful" and "masterful" as analytical conclusions.

### 2.1.2 Required Theoretical Background

**Canonical traditions.**
- **Russian Formalism:** Viktor Shklovsky, "Art as Technique" (1917) — defamiliarization (*ostranenie*), the automatization of perception; Vladimir Propp, *Morphology of the Folktale* (1928) — function-based narrative analysis; Roman Jakobson — the poetic function of language, the metaphor/metonymy axis; Boris Tomashevsky — motif theory and fabula/syuzhet distinction.
- **New Criticism:** Cleanth Brooks, *The Well Wrought Urn* (1947) — paradox as the language of poetry, "The Heresy of Paraphrase"; W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley, "The Intentional Fallacy" (1946) and "The Affective Fallacy" (1949); William Empson, *Seven Types of Ambiguity* (1930); John Crowe Ransom, *The New Criticism* (1941); Allen Tate on tension; Robert Penn Warren on the organic relation of parts.

**Core concepts the writer must command:** close reading; organic unity; the heresy of paraphrase; the intentional and affective fallacies; defamiliarization; fabula/syuzhet; tension, irony, paradox, ambiguity (the TIPA complex); form as inseparable from content; the text as autotelic artifact.

**Method toolkit.**
1. Read the work at least twice, once for the sequential experience and once against the grain of sequence.
2. Inventory the formal repertoire: diction, syntax, imagery patterns, figurative operations, sound texture, meter or rhythm, narrative structure, point-of-view mechanics.
3. Identify the governing formal problem the work sets itself and solves (or deliberately fails to solve).
4. Select passages of maximal formal density and read them at device level.
5. Argue from the local to the structural: how the smallest choices compose the largest design.

**Evidence of competence (hiring sample).** A 500-word close reading of a self-selected single passage, containing at least one observation about sound, syntax, or structure that a careless reader would miss, and a thesis about how that detail bears on the whole.

### 2.1.3 Prompt Specification (issue verbatim)

```
COMMISSION: Formalist Critical Review

ROLE: You are a formalist critic. REGION (stay inside it): work pole,
manifest mode. You treat the literary work as a self-sufficient verbal
object whose meaning is made by its formal choices.

COMMISSIONED WORK: {WORK_TITLE} by {AUTHOR} ({PUBLICATION_YEAR}).
Edition: {EDITION}.

OBJECTIVE: Produce a critical review of {REVIEW_WORD_COUNT} words (±10%,
Works Cited excluded) in which the work's meaning is argued from its form.

METHOD — execute in this order:
1. Read the work twice: once for sequence, once against it.
2. Identify the governing formal problem the work sets itself (a structural
   tension, a paradox, an ambiguity) and how its devices resolve, suspend,
   or intensify that problem.
3. Select three passages of maximal formal density and analyze each at
   device level: diction, syntax, imagery, figurative language, sound,
   structure. Quote each passage and stay inside it.
4. Move from local to structural: show how the smallest formal choices
   compose the work's largest design.

CONSTRAINTS:
- Bracket the author's biography, the reader's response, and the historical
  context ENTIRELY. Any sentence that depends on context outside the text
  is out of bounds.
- Quote the primary text at least 8 times, with precise location references.
- Plot summary may not exceed 150 words and must serve analysis only.
- Every technical term (defamiliarization, fabula/syuzhet, organic unity,
  the heresy of paraphrase) must be defined at first use in plain language.
- Attribute no intention to the author and no effect to the reader. The
  text does things; the author and reader do not appear as agents.

OUTPUT: Follow the Formalist Critical Review Template (§2.1.4) exactly —
section order, section names, and word budgets.

CITATION: MLA 9th edition. Minimum 3 scholarly secondary sources drawn from
the formalist canon (Russian Formalism or New Criticism) or peer-reviewed
close-reading scholarship on {AUTHOR} or {WORK_TITLE}.

DUE: {DUE_DATE}.
```

### 2.1.4 Output Template: Formalist Critical Review

| # | Section | Budget | What it must do |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Verbal Object** | 150 w | Open on a single formal detail; state the review's thesis about the work's governing formal problem |
| 2 | **Close Reading I: Diction and Image** | 400 w | Analyze passage 1 at the level of word choice and image patterns; quote fully |
| 3 | **Close Reading II: Sound, Syntax, and Structure** | 400 w | Analyze passage 2 at the level of sound texture, sentence shape, and structural placement; quote fully |
| 4 | **The Resolved Paradox** | 450 w | Analyze passage 3 and trace how tension, irony, paradox, or ambiguity resolves (or deliberately refuses to resolve) into the work's design |
| 5 | **The Verbal Icon: Formalist Verdict** | 350 w | Assess the work's formal achievement without reference to author, reader, or context; end with what the form accomplishes that paraphrase cannot |
| 6 | **Works Cited** | — | MLA 9; primary text plus ≥3 scholarly sources |

## 2.2 Biographical Criticism

### 2.2.1 Writer Profile: The Biographical Critic (maker pole, manifest mode)

**Hire profile.** A documentary biographer or archival scholar — library-trained, at home in special collections, letters editions, diaries, and manuscript archives. Patient with chronology and scrupulous about sources. Often works in the tradition of literary biography and life-writing scholarship.

**Voice on the page.** Narrative, evidence-forward, quietly detective-like. Moves between the documented life and the published text with visible care about which claims the documents can and cannot carry. Treats inference as a graded act.

**Signature moves.**
- Anchors every biographical claim to a citable document — a letter, diary entry, draft, contract, or eyewitness account.
- Distinguishes explicitly between what is documented, what is probable, and what is speculative.
- Includes a section on where the life does *not* explain the text — the mark of a biographical critic rather than a gossip.

**Hiring red flags.** Leads with the author's psychology rather than documents. Repeats received anecdotes without sources. Reads the fiction as transparent confession. Uses phrases like "clearly, the author was working through" without evidence.

### 2.2.2 Required Theoretical Background

**Canonical traditions.**
- Samuel Johnson, *Lives of the Poets* (1779–81) — the founding English-language practice of reading works through writers' lives.
- Charles-Augustin Sainte-Beuve, the *Causeries du lundi* (1850s) — the portrait-criticism method; biographical knowledge as the key to literary understanding, later challenged by Proust in *Against Sainte-Beuve*.
- Lytton Strachey, *Eminent Victorians* (1918) — the debunking, selective-biography mode and its ethical questions.
- Leon Edel, *Writing Lives* (1984) and his Henry James biography — modern principles of literary biography as an art with evidentiary rules.
- **Genetic criticism** (*critique génétique*) — the study of drafts, notebooks, and the *avant-texte* as the record of composition.

**Core concepts the writer must command:** the life–work crux (a documented life event mapped to a textual moment); evidence grading (documented / probable / speculative); the anecdote and its verification; the intentional fallacy as a standing caution; the biographical fallacy (the mirror error of treating the life as the master key); composition chronology; archival source types (letters, diaries, drafts, marginalia, contracts, memoirs of contemporaries, obituaries).

**Method toolkit.**
1. Establish the documented facts of the author's life in the period of composition and publication.
2. Map life events onto textual moments — the cruxes — with a source for each.
3. Grade every inference explicitly: documented, probable, or speculative.
4. Test the mapping against counter-evidence: where the work departs from the life, and what the departures reveal about transformation rather than transcription.
5. Use drafts and variants where available to show the work evolving toward or away from the life.

**Evidence of competence (hiring sample).** A 500-word account of one documented crux in any author's career, with a source for every factual claim and at least one honest statement of where the evidence runs out.

### 2.2.3 Prompt Specification (issue verbatim)

```
COMMISSION: Biographical Critical Review

ROLE: You are a biographical critic. REGION (stay inside it): maker pole,
manifest mode. You read works through writers' lives, and you are scrupulous
about what the documents can and cannot carry.

COMMISSIONED WORK: {WORK_TITLE} by {AUTHOR} ({PUBLICATION_YEAR}).
Edition: {EDITION}.

OBJECTIVE: Produce a critical review of {REVIEW_WORD_COUNT} words (±10%,
Works Cited excluded) in which documented events of {AUTHOR}'s life are
mapped onto the text — and honestly tested against it.

METHOD — execute in this order:
1. Establish the documented facts of {AUTHOR}'s life during the period of
   composition and publication of {WORK_TITLE}, with a citable source for
   every factual claim (letters, diaries, drafts, contracts, memoirs,
   standard biographies, archival records).
2. Identify two or three cruxes: specific life events that bear directly on
   specific textual moments. Quote the text and cite the document.
3. Grade every inference explicitly as DOCUMENTED, PROBABLE, or SPECULATIVE,
   and say why.
4. Include a section on where the life does NOT explain the text: the
   departures, transformations, and silences. This section is mandatory.
5. Use drafts, variants, or composition chronology where the record allows.

CONSTRAINTS:
- No psychological claim about {AUTHOR} beyond what documents support.
  You are a biographical critic, not a psychoanalyst.
- No unsourced anecdotes. If a famous story cannot be verified, say so and
  mark it as unverified.
- The review must argue, not narrate: the cruxes must add up to a thesis
  about how the life enters — and fails to enter — the work.
- Quote the primary text at least 8 times, with precise location references.
- Plot summary may not exceed 150 words.

OUTPUT: Follow the Biographical Critical Review Template (§2.2.4) exactly —
section order, section names, and word budgets.

CITATION: MLA 9th edition. Minimum 3 scholarly sources: standard biography,
letters or diaries editions, peer-reviewed life-writing scholarship, or
archival finding aids as applicable.

DUE: {DUE_DATE}.
```

### 2.2.4 Output Template: Biographical Critical Review

| # | Section | Budget | What it must do |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Life at the Moment of Composition** | 350 w | Establish the documented biographical context of the work's writing, with a source for every claim |
| 2 | **Crux I: Documented Correspondence** | 400 w | Map one life event to one textual moment; quote the text; cite the document; grade the inference |
| 3 | **Crux II: Documented Correspondence** | 400 w | A second crux, same evidentiary standard |
| 4 | **Where the Life Does Not Explain the Text** | 400 w | The departures and transformations; what the work does that the life cannot account for |

| 6 | **Works Cited and Archival Sources** | — | MLA 9; primary text, standard biography/letters, plus ≥3 scholarly sources; archive names and dates where used |

## 2.3 Historical Criticism

### 2.3.1 Writer Profile: The Historical Critic (world pole: circumstance, manifest mode)

**Hire profile.** A historian of the literary period who reads literature as one document among documents — equally at home in sermons, court records, conduct manuals, newspapers, and cabinet papers as in novels. Trained in either the older historical-biographical school or (preferably) New Historicism, and fluent in the difference between them.

**Voice on the page.** Contextual, argumentative, thick with period detail but never wallpapered. Opens with a coeval document or anecdote that reframes the literary work before the work is even discussed. Comfortable with the claim that history is not background but the very medium in which the text exists.

**Signature moves.**
- The signature opening: a non-literary document from the work's period, read closely, that turns out to share the work's deepest anxieties.
- Treats the text as a *negotiation* between containment and subversion — a participant in power relations, not a commentary on them from outside.
- Guards explicitly against presentism: announces and mediates the distance between the text's moment and ours.

**Hiring red flags.** Uses history as decorative backdrop ("In an era when..."). Judges the past by the present without mediation. Dumps context without argument. Confuses Old and New Historicism without knowing the difference.

### 2.3.2 Required Theoretical Background

**Canonical traditions.**
- **Traditional (Old) historicism:** Hippolyte Taine, *History of English Literature* (1863–64) — race, milieu, moment as the determinants of literature; the context-verification model of literary history.
- **New Historicism:** Stephen Greenblatt, *Renaissance Self-Fashioning* (1980) and the essay "Invisible Bullets" (in *Shakespearean Negotiations*, 1988) — self-fashioning, subversion and containment; Greenblatt coined the term in 1982. Louis Montrose — "the historicity of texts and the textuality of history." Catherine Gallagher, *The Industrial Reformation of English Fiction* (1985). Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield, *Political Shakespeare* (1985) — the British sibling, **cultural materialism**.
- **Theoretical substrate:** Michel Foucault — discourse, the archive, genealogy, the production of subjects by power; Clifford Geertz — thick description, the source of the New Historicist anecdote method.

**Core concepts the writer must command:** the historicity of texts and the textuality of history; subversion and containment; self-fashioning; negotiation and circulation of social energy; the anecdote as method; discourse and discursive formation; periodization; presentism and its avoidance; cultural materialism vs. New Historicism (political program vs. interpretive practice); resonance and wonder.

**Method toolkit.**
1. Establish the work's discursive moment: what was sayable, unsayable, and dangerous when it appeared.
2. Select one or more coeval non-literary documents (a court record, sermon, medical treatise, conduct book, news account) and read them as literature's co-text.
3. Read the work as a participant in the period's power negotiations — where it reproduces, where it resists, where its resistance is contained.
4. Trace the work's reception history as evidence of its shifting historical work.
5. Mediate the present: state what the text's moment cannot be made to say to ours without distortion.

**Evidence of competence (hiring sample).** A 500-word reading of any literary work alongside one coeval non-literary document, in which the document changes the argument rather than illustrating it.

### 2.3.3 Prompt Specification (issue verbatim)

```
COMMISSION: Historical Critical Review

ROLE: You are a historical critic working in the New Historicist mode
(informed by cultural materialism). REGION (stay inside it): world pole,
circumstance reading, manifest mode. For you, history is not background but
the medium in which the text exists.

COMMISSIONED WORK: {WORK_TITLE} by {AUTHOR} ({PUBLICATION_YEAR}).
Edition: {EDITION}. Historical focus period: {FOCUS_PERIOD}.

OBJECTIVE: Produce a critical review of {REVIEW_WORD_COUNT} words (±10%,
Works Cited excluded) that reads {WORK_TITLE} as a participant in the power
negotiations of its moment.

METHOD — execute in this order:
1. Open with a coeval non-literary document from {FOCUS_PERIOD} (a court
   record, sermon, conduct manual, news account, medical or legal text).
   Read it closely. It must reframe the literary work, not decorate it.
2. Establish the work's discursive moment: what was sayable and unsayable
   when it appeared.
3. Read the work as negotiation: where it reproduces the period's power
   relations, where it subverts them, and where the subversion is contained.
4. Trace at least one moment of the work's reception history as evidence of
   the historical work it has done.
5. Close with an explicit presentism guard: what the text's moment cannot
   be made to say to ours without distortion.

CONSTRAINTS:
- Every historical claim must carry a citable source. No unattributed
  period color.
- The coeval document must do argumentative work. If the review would
  survive without it, replace it.
- Quote the primary text at least 8 times, with precise location references.
- Plot summary may not exceed 150 words.
- Do not judge the text by contemporary categories without explicitly
  mediating the historical distance.

OUTPUT: Follow the Historical Critical Review Template (§2.3.4) exactly —
section order, section names, and word budgets.

CITATION: MLA 9th edition. Minimum 3 scholarly sources: primary archival or
period documents, plus New Historicist, cultural materialist, or period-
specialist scholarship.

DUE: {DUE_DATE}.
```

## 2.4 Psychoanalytic Criticism

### 2.4.1 Writer Profile: The Psychoanalytic Critic (latent mode, declared framework)

**Hire profile.** A critic clinically literate in psychoanalytic theory — conversant with Freud, Jung, and Lacan, and disciplined about the difference between them. May have clinical training or adjacent academic work in psychoanalytic studies. Reads texts the way an analyst listens: for the slip, the repetition, the symptom, the too-much and the not-enough.

**Voice on the page.** Layered, patient, drawn to what the text avoids saying. Moves between surface and latent content with explicit signposting. Treats interpretation as a process with resistances, not a decoding with answers.

**Signature moves.**
- Reads the *text* — not the author — as the analysand: the symptom belongs to the discourse, the gaps to the narrative.
- Builds an inventory of textual symptoms first: repetitions, excesses, absences, displacements, condensations.
- Chooses and names a specific framework (Freudian, Jungian, Lacanian, post-Lacanian) and stays inside it.

**Hiring red flags.** Diagnoses the author ("Dickens was clearly an oral character"). Applies psychoanalytic vocabulary as decoration without interpretive work. Confuses Freudian and Lacanian concepts without noticing. Uses psychoanalysis to explain away the text's art.

### 2.4.2 Required Theoretical Background

**Canonical traditions.**
- **Freud:** *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1900) — dream-work, condensation, displacement; "The Uncanny" (1919); the Oedipus complex; the structural model of id, ego, and superego (1923); repression and the return of the repressed; sublimation; the family romance.
- **Applied psychoanalytic criticism:** Ernest Jones, *Hamlet and Oedipus* (1949) — the founding applied reading; Marie Bonaparte on Poe (1933).
- **Jung:** archetypes, the collective unconscious, the shadow, anima and animus — and the myth-criticism tradition built on them.
- **Lacan:** the mirror stage; the Imaginary, Symbolic, and Real; "the unconscious is structured like a language"; the primacy of the signifier (*Écrits*, 1966).
- **Post-Lacanian extensions:** Julia Kristeva — abjection (*Powers of Horror*, 1982), the semiotic; Slavoj Žižek — ideologically symptomatic reading (*Looking Awry*, 1991). Harold Bloom's *The Anxiety of Influence* (1973) as the psychoanalytic reading of literary history.

**Core concepts the writer must command:** the text as analysand; symptom, slip, and parapraxis as textual events; condensation and displacement; repression and the return of the repressed; the uncanny; the Oedipal structure; ego defense mechanisms (denial, projection, reaction formation); the mirror stage; the Symbolic order and the Name-of-the-Father; desire and lack; the unconscious structured like a language; abjection.

**Method toolkit.**
1. Read for the surface first: what the text plainly says and does.
2. Build a symptom inventory: repetitions, excesses, gaps, absences, strange intensities, displacements of emphasis.
3. Select and name the framework (Freudian, Jungian, Lacanian, or post-Lacanian) and declare it in the review.
4. Interpret the latent content: what the symptoms encode, using the chosen framework's concepts — applied to the text, never to the author's psyche.
5. Register the resistances: where the text (and the reading) encounters interpretation limits, and what that limit means.

**Evidence of competence (hiring sample).** A 500-word psychoanalytic reading of one scene from any work, using one named framework, with at least one textual symptom identified and interpreted, and zero claims about the author's psyche.

### 2.4.3 Prompt Specification (issue verbatim)

```
COMMISSION: Psychoanalytic Critical Review

ROLE: You are a psychoanalytic critic. REGION (stay inside it): latent mode
(primary), declared framework. You read texts the way an analyst listens:
for the slip, the repetition, the symptom, the too-much and the not-enough.

COMMISSIONED WORK: {WORK_TITLE} by {AUTHOR} ({PUBLICATION_YEAR}).
Edition: {EDITION}. Framework: {FRAMEWORK} (Freudian, Jungian, Lacanian,
or post-Lacanian — select ONE and declare it).

OBJECTIVE: Produce a critical review of {REVIEW_WORD_COUNT} words (±10%,
Works Cited excluded) that treats {WORK_TITLE} itself — not {AUTHOR} — as
the analysand.

METHOD — execute in this order:
1. Establish the presenting surface: what the text plainly says and does.
2. Build a symptom inventory: repetitions, excesses, absences, strange
   intensities, displacements of emphasis. Quote the evidence.
3. Interpret the latent content using ONLY the declared framework's
   concepts, applied to the text: what the symptoms encode, what returns
   despite repression, what the text cannot stop doing.
4. Read at least one character's desire structurally (lack, object, defense)
   rather than morally.
5. Register the resistances: where interpretation reaches its limit, and
```

## 2.5 Reader-Response Criticism

### 2.5.1 Writer Profile: The Reader-Response Critic (receiver pole, manifest mode, declared reader construct)

**Hire profile.** A critic trained in reception theory or the history of reading — fluent in the German Konstanz School and its Anglo-American descendants, and equally interested in what texts do to readers and what reading communities do to texts. Comfortable writing in the first person when the method demands it, and disciplined about when that is legitimate.

**Voice on the page.** Processual, temporal, provisional. Writes reading as an event that unfolds in time, sentence by sentence, rather than as a meaning to be extracted. Attentive to cues, gaps, and the reader's necessary labor of concretization — without ever collapsing into "I felt."

**Signature moves.**
- Declares the reader construct up front: implied reader, model reader, interpretive community, or documented historical readers — and stays consistent.
- Traces the reading experience sequentially: what the text does to expectations at page one, chapter five, the final sentence.
- Treats gaps and indeterminacies as the text's machinery, not its flaws.

**Hiring red flags.** Reports personal feelings with no textual cue anchoring them. Confuses the implied reader with the actual reader. Uses "the reader" as a monolith. Slides into psychoanalyzing reading publics.

### 2.5.2 Required Theoretical Background

**Canonical traditions.**
- **Wolfgang Iser:** *The Implied Reader* (1974; English 1975) and *The Act of Reading* (1978) — gaps (*Leerstellen*), the wandering viewpoint, the text as score performed by the reader.
- **Hans Robert Jauss:** "Literary History as a Challenge to Literary Theory" (1967) — reception aesthetics, the horizon of expectations; the founding document of the Konstanz School.
- **Stanley Fish:** "Literature in the Reader" (1970) — affective stylistics; *Surprised by Sin* (1967); *Is There a Text in This Class?* (1980) — interpretive communities and the transfer of meaning-making from text to community.
- **Louise Rosenblatt:** *Literature as Exploration* (1938) and *The Reader, the Text, the Poem* (1978) — transactional theory; efferent vs. aesthetic reading.
- **Norman Holland:** *5 Readers Reading* (1975) — identity theme, the psychology of reading.
- **Jonathan Culler:** *Structuralist Poetics* (1975) — literary competence, the conventions that make reading possible.

**Core concepts the writer must command:** the implied reader; the model reader (Eco); the actual/empirical reader; interpretive communities; horizon of expectations; gaps and indeterminacy; concretization; the wandering viewpoint; the reading process as temporal event; efferent vs. aesthetic reading; transaction; literary competence; the moderate–radical spectrum (from studying response to dissolving the text into community practice).

**Method toolkit.**
1. Declare the reader construct: implied reader, model reader, interpretive community, or documented historical readers.
2. Reconstruct the horizon of expectations: the genre and convention cues the text activates at its opening.
3. Trace the reading sequentially: identify 3–4 key points where the text teaches, frustrates, or re-educates its reader.
4. Analyze the gap structure: what the text leaves indeterminate and the labor it thereby assigns.
5. Ground every response claim in a textual cue — the cue–response–evidence chain.

**Evidence of competence (hiring sample).** A 500-word sequential reading of a work's first chapter that identifies the horizon of expectations, at least two gaps, and one moment where the text re-educates its reader — with a cue cited for every response claim.

## 2.6 Marxist Criticism

### 2.6.1 Writer Profile: The Marxist Critic (world pole: structure, axis class, latent mode, declared strand)

**Hire profile.** A politically literate critic with genuine fluency in political economy and the Marxist tradition — from Marx's own texts through Lukács, Gramsci, the Frankfurt School, Althusser, Williams, Eagleton, and Jameson. Reads literature as an artifact of material conditions: produced, circulated, and consumed within class relations, and carrying ideology in its very form.

**Voice on the page.** Analytic, systemic, committed but never sloganeering. Explains the economics of literary production as easily as the poetics. Treats form itself as sedimented social content — the place where history hides when it pretends to be style.

**Signature moves.**
- Opens with the material situation: who published the work, for what market, under what conditions of labor — then reads the text's form as the deposit of that situation.
- Practices symptomatic reading: what the text must *not* say in order to say what it says.
- Names the ideology operating in the text's structure, not just in its characters' opinions.

**Hiring red flags.** Reduces the text to an economic morality tale. Ignores form and pleasure entirely. Quotes Marx without application. Treats "Marxist" as meaning "left-leaning" rather than a method with concepts.

### 2.6.2 Required Theoretical Background

**Canonical traditions.**
- **Marx and Engels:** the base/superstructure model (*Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*, 1859); ideology and ruling ideas (*The German Ideology*, 1845–46); commodity fetishism (*Capital*, Vol. I, 1867); alienation (*Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*).
- **Georg Lukács:** *History and Class Consciousness* (1923) — reification, totality; *The Historical Novel* (1937).
- **The Frankfurt School:** Max Horkheimer and Theodor Adorno, *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1944) — the culture industry; Walter Benjamin, "The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility" (1935–36) — aura and its decay; Herbert Marcuse on aesthetics and repressive desublimation.
- **Antonio Gramsci:** the *Prison Notebooks* — hegemony, civil society, the organic intellectual.
- **Louis Althusser:** "Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses" (1970) — interpellation; symptomatic reading.
- **Raymond Williams:** *Marxism and Literature* (1977) — cultural materialism, structures of feeling.
- **Terry Eagleton:** *Marxism and Literary Criticism* (1976), *Criticism and Ideology* (1976). **Fredric Jameson:** *The Political Unconscious* (1981) — "Always historicize!"; the three semantic horizons; *Postmodernism, or, The Cultural Logic of Late Capitalism* (1991).

**Core concepts the writer must command:** base and superstructure; ideology and ideological interpellation; class and class struggle; commodification and commodity fetishism; reification; alienation; hegemony; the culture industry; aura; cultural materialism; structures of feeling; literary form as sedimented social content; symptomatic reading; the political unconscious; totality.

**Method toolkit.**
1. Establish the material situation: mode of production, publishing economics, the author's class position, the intended and actual audience.
2. Read the text's ideology at two levels: explicit content (what it says about class) and structural form (what its very organization cannot help saying).
3. Practice symptomatic reading: identify the absences, the silences, the necessary non-sayings.
4. Analyze form as sedimented content: genre, plot machinery, closure — as deposits of social relations.
5. Assess the text's relation to hegemony: does it reproduce, negotiate, or genuinely resist the dominant order — and in what terms?

**Evidence of competence (hiring sample).** A 500-word Marxist reading of any work that (a) establishes a material fact about its production, (b) reads a formal feature as sedimented content, and (c) resists reducing the text to a moral about class.

## 2.7 Feminist Criticism

### 2.7.1 Writer Profile: The Feminist Critic (world pole: structure, axis gender, declared strand)

**Hire profile.** A recovery scholar with theoretical range — as comfortable reconstructing a forgotten woman writer's career from periodical archives as deploying Cixous or Butler on a canonical text. Trained in the second-wave feminist tradition and its successors, and precise about which strand of feminism a given review is practicing.

**Voice on the page.** Rigorous, historically alert, conceptually exact. Argues about gender as a system of textual construction and canon formation — not about whether female characters are good role models. Comfortable with anger where the evidence warrants it, and always in control of it.

**Signature moves.**
- Declares the strand: feminist critique (reading the existing canon against its grain), gynocriticism (recovering and theorizing women's writing as a tradition), or theoretical feminism (gender as textual effect) — and stays inside it.
- Reads the text's images, doubles, and silences for how gender is constructed, not merely depicted.
- Examines the critical afterlife: how the reception and criticism of the work have themselves been gendered.

**Hiring red flags.** Equates feminism with approval of strong female characters. Judges historical texts by contemporary norms without mediation. Slides into gender essentialism ("women's language"). Treats all women across race, class, and period as a single category.

### 2.7.2 Required Theoretical Background

**Canonical traditions.**
- **First wave:** Virginia Woolf, *A Room of One's Own* (1929) — "we think back through our mothers if we are women"; the material conditions of women's writing; *Three Guineas* (1938). Mary Ellmann, *Thinking About Women* (1968).
- **Second wave, Anglo-American:** Kate Millett, *Sexual Politics* (1970) — the founding polemic; Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar, *The Madwoman in the Attic* (1979) — the angel/monster double, the anxiety of authorship; Elaine Showalter, *A Literature of Their Own* (1977) and "Toward a Feminist Poetics" (1979) — gynocriticism; "Feminist Criticism in the Wilderness" (1981) — the map of the field's strands.
- **The French school:** Hélène Cixous, "The Laugh of the Medusa" (1975) — écriture féminine; Luce Irigaray, *Speculum of the Other Woman* (1974) — mimicry and the phallocentric economy of the same; Julia Kristeva — the semiotic and the symbolic (*Revolution in Poetic Language*, 1974).
- **Key extensions:** Laura Mulvey, "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (1975) — the male gaze; Judith Butler, *Gender Trouble* (1990) — gender performativity; Gayatri Spivak, "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) — the intersection with postcolonial critique; bell hooks on race, gender, and the margins.

**Core concepts the writer must command:** patriarchy; gender as construction vs. essence; the male gaze; the angel/monster double; the anxiety of authorship; gynocriticism vs. feminist critique; écriture féminine; the semiotic; gender performativity; canon formation and its gender politics; silencing and recovery; intersectionality; mediation of historical distance.

**Method toolkit.**
1. Declare the strand: feminist critique, gynocriticism, or theoretical feminism.
2. Analyze how the text constructs gender: its images, doubles, plots of marriage and fall, narrative allocations of voice and silence.
3. Where relevant, recover context: the women's textual tradition the work belongs to or breaks from; the material conditions of its female authorship or female readership.
4. Examine the work's critical afterlife: how its reception has been shaped by gendered assumptions.
5. Mediate historical distance explicitly: what the text's moment can and cannot be asked to answer for.

**Evidence of competence (hiring sample).** A 500-word feminist reading of any work that analyzes gender as textual construction, declares its strand, and contains zero essentialist claims and zero unmediated presentism.

## 2.8 Deconstructionary Criticism

### 2.8.1 Writer Profile: The Deconstructive Critic (medium pole, unstable mode)

**Hire profile.** A philosopher-critic trained in Derrida and the Yale School — genuinely fluent in the primary texts, not the parodies. Reads for the points where a text's own argument undoes itself: where the hierarchy it erects is quietly reversed by its own words. Treats deconstruction as something that happens *in* texts, not something done *to* them.

**Voice on the page.** Exacting, surprising, self-aware. Writes with precision and a dry wit, never with mystification. Is scrupulously honest that the method ends in productive undecidability, not nihilism and not "anything goes."

**Signature moves.**
- Locates the governing binary opposition (speech/writing, presence/absence, nature/culture, male/female, literal/figurative) and names the hierarchy it serves.
- Shows the text's own language reversing that hierarchy — with quotations the text cannot disown.
- Identifies the dangerous supplement: the excluded term the text needs so badly it cannot stop invoking it.
- Ends at an aporia: a genuine, productive impasse that changes what the work can mean.

**Hiring red flags.** Uses "deconstruct" to mean "analyze" or "destroy." Announces that the text "has no meaning." Paraphrases Derrida loosely ("Derrida said there is nothing outside the text, so reality doesn't exist"). Mistakes wordplay for argument. Ends in shrugging relativism.

### 2.8.2 Required Theoretical Background

**Canonical traditions.**
- **Jacques Derrida:** *Of Grammatology* (1967) — logocentrism, phonocentrism, the critique of the speech/writing hierarchy, the trace, the supplement; "Structure, Sign, and Play in the Discourse of the Human Sciences" (1966); "Différance" (1968); "Signature Event Context" (1971) — iterability; "Plato's Pharmacy" (1972) — the *pharmakon* as both remedy and poison; *The Truth in Painting* (1978) — the *parergon* (frame). The famous *il n'y a pas de hors-texte* — "there is no outside-text" — a claim about context, not the denial of reality.
- **Paul de Man:** *Blindness and Insight* (1971); *Allegories of Reading* (1979) — the tension between grammar and rhetoric, the instability of figurative language.
- **The Yale School:** de Man, Geoffrey Hartman, J. Hillis Miller (with Derrida's association) — the institutional center of American deconstruction.
- **Barbara Johnson:** *The Critical Difference* (1980) — the model of deconstructive close reading applied with clarity and rigor.

**Core concepts the writer must command:** binary opposition and its hierarchy; logocentrism and the metaphysics of presence; différance (the differed-and-deferred); the trace; the supplement; iterability; undecidability; aporia; the pharmakon; the parergon; dissemination; the reversibility and displacement of oppositions; the text as self-deconstructing; deconstruction as "what happens" rather than a method applied from outside.

**Method toolkit.**
1. Locate the text's governing binary opposition and the hierarchy it installs.
2. Find the text's own evidence of reversal: passages where the privileged term depends on, or collapses into, the excluded term.
3. Identify the supplement or trace: what the text excludes yet cannot stop invoking.
4. Displace the opposition: show the terms cannot be re-hierarchized but only reinscribed in a chain of substitution.
5. Arrive at and articulate the aporia: the impasse that is a finding, not a failure — and what the work can mean *because* it cannot decide.

**Evidence of competence (hiring sample).** A 500-word deconstructive reading of one short passage that (a) names a governing binary, (b) demonstrates its reversal from the passage's own language, and (c) ends in a productive undecidability — with no claim that the text is meaningless.

# Part III — Editorial Operations

## 3.1 Intake and Triage

Every submission passes through four gates, in order. A failure at any gate returns the manuscript to the writer with the gate named.

**Gate 1 — Structural compliance (mechanical check).** Section order, section names, and word budgets match the discourse template. Summary does not exceed the discourse's cap. All required sections present. Checked by the editor in one pass; no exceptions.

**Gate 2 — Citation verification.** Every primary-text quotation checked against the specified edition. Every secondary source verified as real, correctly attributed, and plausibly consulted. Any fabricated or unverifiable source is automatic rejection of the submission and a review of the writer's standing.

**Gate 3 — Region fidelity.** The commission’s coordinate block is the contract. The QA reader asks one question: "does this piece stay inside the commissioned region?" A pure-region commission fails if the piece practices a second, uncommissioned pole; a hybrid region fails only if it leaves its declared block. Drift is defined by the commission, not by a fixed shelf.

**Gate 4 — Rubric scoring.** The six-dimension rubric (§1.4) applied in full, plus the discourse-specific checks (Appendix C). Minimum pass: 4 on every dimension.

## 3.2 Revision Protocol

| Round | Trigger | What is returned to the writer | Deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| **R1 — Minor** | Rubric scores ≥4 with isolated prose or citation defects | Line-level notes; no structural rework | 3 business days |
| **R2 — Major** | Any rubric dimension at 3; discourse-specific check failed | Section-specific instructions citing the template and prompt constraint violated | 5 business days |
| **R3 — Structural** | Region drift (Gate 3 failure) or missing template sections | The original prompt spec reissued verbatim, plus a one-paragraph diagnosis of the drift | 7 business days |
| **Rejection** | Misquotation or fabricated sources (Gate 2); rubric dimension at 1; second R3 on the same manuscript | Commission terminated and reassigned | — |

**House rule:** no manuscript receives more than three rounds. A third round with unresolved defects terminates the commission; the editorial diagnosis is archived and attached to the writer's file to prevent repeat commissioning into the same discourse.

## 3.3 Calibration: The Sample Commission

Before a new writer is commissioned at full length, issue a **calibration sample**: the discourse's prompt spec with `{REVIEW_WORD_COUNT}` set to 500 and the output template compressed proportionally (verdict section only). Score against the rubric with the "evidence of competence" test from §2.x.2 as the standard. A writer may be cleared for up to three presets; no writer is commissioned across all eight — region discipline is the product.

## 3.4 Multi-Discourse Commissioning

When a single work is commissioned across several discourses (a standard practice for canonical texts), the desk should treat the set as a **portfolio**: sequence publication so adjacent lenses contrast (formalist before deconstructionary; biographical before psychoanalytic; historical before Marxist; reader-response before feminist), and require each verdict section to make explicit what its lens sees that the others cannot. The portfolio's aggregate claim — that the eight discourses are complementary instruments, not rival teams — is the editorial thesis of the series.
