# Panel Review: "THE WALLS —" (the Minotaur poem)

Part I reviews the final draft alone. Part II rereads it against the four earlier drafts and corrects Part I where the drafts prove it wrong.

# Part I: The Final Draft

Five critics, each set to a different coordinate block from the Discourse About Art framework, so their reviews overlap as little as possible. Each one names its standard before giving a verdict. After the reviews: where they agree, where they disagree, and questions for revision.

## 1. The Prosodist

The most important thing about this poem doesn't show on the surface: it's built in syllables. Take away the frame words and nearly every line has four syllables: "OH, BEAST! NO MORE" / "MAZE! TWAS YOUR OLD" / "GRAVE — YOUR HOME, NOT…" / "YET STILL LIFE STILL." The caps stanzas are sets of four-beat bricks, and the stanzas open or close on a single monosyllable: "STONES." "FACE." "FIRST:" … "THEN." "DEAR" … "HOPE" … "FOND?" A poem about walls is made of evenly sized blocks with a capstone on each course. Whether you planned it or heard it, it holds up, and it's the strongest structural decision in the draft.

Punctuation works as architecture too. The spaced colons ("JUST : SEALS SOWN," "THEIR SEAL : GONE.") read less like grammar and more like ratio notation, A : B. They make each line a balance or an equation, which fits a speaker obsessed with fact and myth as convertible terms. The dashes act as the walls between rooms.

Two formal events are excellent. First, the lowercase voice is one quotation stretched across the whole poem. It opens with “…you— and doesn't close until “…know. no no.” Read alone, it becomes one sentence: you… you don't… don't want to… know. no no. Second, the caps voice opens a parenthesis in the last stanza, "(WE / HOPE," and never closes it. The quiet voice finishes its sentence and the loud voice can't. That reversal is the poem's argument, carried out in punctuation.

Testing points: the grace / place / embrace / face rhyme run in stanza four is the one place where sound turns automatic. Four full rhymes in six short lines makes the stanza chime when the rest of the poem grinds. "ROAM AGAIN — HELL NOW" is the only five-syllable line in the stanza grid. If that's intentional (the line strays out of the maze), keep it. If not, it's the one brick out of true.

Verdict: formally serious and more controlled than it looks at first. Almost all of the devices do work.

## 2. The Mythographer

Minotaur poems usually take one of two seats: Theseus's (the heroic kill) or the beast's (the sympathetic monster, which is mostly Borges's territory since "The House of Asterion," where the creature welcomes his "redeemer" and "scarcely defended himself"). This poem takes neither. Its speaker stands among the rubble after the walls have come down, talking to a Minotaur who is still alive and now has nowhere to be. "FOR WHERE THEN NOW?" isn't a question any version of the myth I know asks. The tradition ends at the kill. This poem starts after the containment ends, and that's a real addition.

The speaker's identity stays open, and that's productive. "THOSE WALLS — THEIR / GRACE — STOOD SIMPLE / IN PLACE, WITHOUT / YOUR EMBRACE" sounds like Daedalus, an architect grieving his structure apart from what it held. Then "WE … WERE ALL SO / SO VERY SO / FOND?" widens to Crete, then to us: the culture that kept the monster because it needed one.

The central chiasmus, "FIRST: / MYTHOLOGIZE / FACT. THEN ONTO: / FACTUALIZE / MYTH.", has a historical match that may help the poem whether you meant it or not. At Knossos, Arthur Evans dug up a real Bronze Age palace, mythologizing fact by calling it the labyrinth and the people "Minoan," and then rebuilt parts of it in concrete to fit the story, factualizing myth. "SEALS," in the first stanza, also suggests Minoan seal-stones, the sealed walls of the labyrinth, the seals broken open in Revelation, and (with "SOWN") Cadmus sowing dragon's teeth that sprout armed men. "JUST : SEALS SOWN" hints that knocking down the walls only scatters the enclosure into new seeds.

"HELL NOW / OR BE IT HELL / THEN" belongs with Marlowe's Mephistopheles ("Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it") and Milton's Satan ("Which way I fly is Hell; myself am Hell"). The labyrinth was never really the prison. The poem knows this and says it in four syllables.

Verdict: a real contribution to a crowded myth. It finds the one moment the tradition skipped.

## 3. The Analyst

The lowercase voice speaks in the exact form of disavowal: "you don't want to know." It's the psychoanalytic formula ("I know very well, but all the same…") turned outward and in pieces. The voice keeps getting interrupted, and every time it comes back it's shorter and more broken: the apostrophe falls out of "don't" between the second and third fragments ("dont want to"), and it ends with "no no," a refusal that's almost a child's.

Whose voice is it? The best reading is that it isn't the Minotaur's. It's the part of the speaker that already knows what the caps voice is working hard not to say. The caps voice performs mastery: commands ("THERE, THERE—FIEND!"), procedures ("FIRST: … THEN ONTO:"), verdicts. The quiet voice stays in the margins, indented at a different depth each time, as if it's moving through corridors. By the last stanza the two have merged. The caps voice starts stammering in the lowercase voice's rhythm: "YOU — NO — / WE … WERE ALL SO / SO VERY SO." "YOU — NO —" echoes "no no." The defense breaks.

"THERE, THERE" is the poem's most exact phrase: comfort and accusation in one breath, the way you'd talk to a frightened animal you also want gone. And "YOUR HOME, NOT… / YET STILL LIFE" holds the whole ambivalence over a line break: "not yet still life" means not yet dead, not yet a picture you can hang. The beast is still alive, and so is the attachment.

The poem leaves room for a reading of shame and concealment (the labyrinth was built to hide a family's shame, Pasiphaë's child), and it's stronger for not pinning that down. "WE WERE ALL SO FOND?" is how families talk about what they hid once it can't be hidden anymore.

Verdict: the psychic drama holds together completely. The two-voice structure is what makes the poem work, and the convergence at the end lands.

## 4. The Workshop Poet

Notes for the next draft, from someone who'd want to steal your stanza grid:

- **Decide the apostrophes on purpose.** "TWAS" is missing its apostrophe, and "dont" loses one that "don't" had two fragments earlier. The second could be the voice wearing down, which is good. The first reads like a slip. If both are intentional, make that clear through consistency. If not, fix "'TWAS."
- **"AND OR."** "STICKS AND OR STEPPED / STONES" is clever: the proverb about words not hurting, plus rubble, plus stepping-stones out. "AND OR" without the slash reads like a hedge in a memo. Try "STICKS AND / STEPPED STONES," or keep "AND/OR" with the slash so the bureaucratic tone is obviously deliberate.
- **Indentation.** The quoted fragments sit at four different depths. If the drift means something (wandering, coming closer), let it go one direction, for example each fragment moving further left toward the caps margin until they meet in the last stanza. Right now it looks arbitrary rather than mapped.
- **Rhyme in stanza four.** grace / place / embrace / face is the place to cut one rhyme. "WITHOUT / YOUR EMBRACE OR / ANY OTHERS' / FACE" could lose "EMBRACE" for a word that pulls against the run.
- **The open parenthesis.** Keep it. Someone will tell you to close it. Don't.
- **Title.** The poem doesn't have one yet. "THE WALLS —" works as a stand-in, but a title naming the aftermath ("After the Labyrinth," "Asterion, Unhoused") would tell readers where they're standing before the first dash.

Verdict: about two revisions from done. The structure is finished. The open choices are surface consistency.

## 5. The Cold Reader

I read this the way someone would if they found it in a magazine with no context.

What holds up: the Minotaur reveal comes late, and the poem earns it. For five stanzas you're addressing "FIEND," "BEAST," "YOU," and naming him only in the last stanza ("DEAR / POOR MINOTAUR") changes everything before it. That's a payoff a cold reader actually gets.

What doesn't: all-caps is a single volume setting, and over eleven stanzas it flattens. When everything is shouted, "THERE, THERE—FIEND!" and "THOSE WALLS — THEIR / GRACE" land at the same pitch, and the reader can't hear the speaker soften. The poem depends on punctuation (colons, dashes, ellipses, quote marks, parentheses) to make the dynamic range the caps take away. Sometimes that's thrilling. Sometimes it feels like being handed a score.

The "MYTHOLOGIZE / FACT … FACTUALIZE / MYTH" stanza is where I felt lectured. It's the one moment the poem states its idea instead of acting it out, and the chiasmus is a little too symmetrical, like a slogan. The other critics find depth in it, but on a first read it's the stanza most likely to lose someone.

The ending is risky. "SO / SO VERY SO / FOND?" will strike some readers as devastating and others as cloying. I was in the first group, narrowly, because the question mark saves it. It turns sentiment into an accusation.

Verdict: difficult, and mostly worth the difficulty. The caps cost more than the poet may think, and the thesis stanza is the weakest point for a first-time reader.

## Where the panel agrees and disagrees

Agreement:
- The two-voice structure is the core achievement: one lowercase sentence spread across the poem, against a caps voice that ends up stammering.
- The unclosed "(WE" parenthesis should stay.
- The late naming of the Minotaur works.
- Setting the poem after the walls fall, rather than at the kill, is new ground for this myth.

Disagreement:
- **The chiasmus stanza.** The Mythographer reads it as the historical and intellectual center (Evans at Knossos). The Cold Reader reads it as the one stanza that explains itself. Both can be true: it may need more strangeness, not less content.
- **All caps.** The Prosodist sees uniform blocks as the right material for a poem about walls. The Cold Reader hears one volume level. A possible test: put one word in the caps voice in lowercase, once, at the moment of convergence.
- **The ending's sentiment.** Three critics say it lands. The Cold Reader says it barely lands.

## Questions for the poet

1. Who is the caps speaker in your mind: Daedalus, Theseus, Minos, a chorus, or someone outside the myth? The poem works without saying, but your answer could settle the "EMBRACE" line.
2. Is the five-syllable "ROAM AGAIN — HELL NOW" a deliberate break from the grid?
3. Are "TWAS" and "dont" intentional erosions?
4. Should the indentation of the quoted fragments follow a path?

## 6. The Textual Scholar (new panelist)

Coordinates: maker pole (the poem as a process) · describe + interpret · standard: does the record support the claim? · reconstructive vantage · testing posture · format: genetic-criticism dossier.

The drafts show a poem that kept its whole set of ideas from the first page and spent four revisions finding their form. Almost every key move in D5 has a traceable origin.

1. **The colon comes from "just as."** D1's "like the seal / you've sown" becomes D3's "WALLS COLLAPSE JUST : / AS SEALS ARE SOWN." The colon sits exactly where the comparison used to be, and D5 then drops "AS." So the Prosodist's ratio reading (A : B) isn't overreading. The colon is a simile compressed into one mark. "JUST" also changes meaning across the drafts: "just as" (at the same moment) becomes "just" (merely).
2. **Seals.** In D1 the seal is something the addressee sowed ("you've sown"), so the Minotaur sealed himself in. In D3, "YOUR" is crossed out above "SEAL" and replaced with "THEIR": the walls stay "YOUR WALLS," but the seal goes to someone else. That split in ownership (your walls, their seal) was a deliberate revision and holds through D5. D1's stanza two also ended "the seal is / Gone?" with a question mark, which later hardens into "GONE."
3. **The margin outline.** D3's green note, WALLS / MINOS / WALLS / MYTH / MINOS, maps onto D5's stanza pattern almost exactly: walls (stanzas 1–2), Minotaur (3), walls (4), myth (5), Minotaur (6). The poem alternates between architecture and inhabitant, with myth as the hinge. "MINOS" may be shorthand for Minotaur, or it may mean the king. If it's the king, Minos becomes the strongest candidate for the caps speaker.
4. **Hill became hell, and Rome was there first.** D2 has "roaming all seven hills." D4 has "ROAMED ALL ROAM / AGAIN — [crossed out] HILL ONE / OR BE IT HILL SEVEN." D5 has "ROAM AGAIN — HELL NOW / OR BE IT HELL / THEN." The first layer is Rome, the city of seven hills, with "ROAM" as a pun on it. The hell layer was a one-vowel revision, and the move from "ONE / SEVEN" to "NOW / THEN" changed a count of places into a span of time. Rome is still in "ROAMED ALL, ROAM AGAIN."
5. **"STEPPED STONES" is the oldest line in the poem.** D1's "a stepping / stone" (a way across) meets D3's "STICKS AND OR STONES" (the proverb about words), and D4 joins them, with "AND OR" added by a caret. "STEPPED" is what's left of D1's stepping-stone.
6. **"STILL LIFE" arrives in D3**, but the "NOT… / YET" line break (your home, not yet still life) appears only in D5.
7. **The Minotaur used to be named early.** D1 ("A minotaur with no maze"), D3 ("OH, MINOTAUR:"), and D4 ("OH, MINOTAUR / OH, BEAST") all name him in stanza three. Holding the name back until "DEAR / POOR MINOTAUR" happens only in D5, which replaces the name with "THERE, THERE—FIEND!" The effect the Cold Reader praised was a late decision.
8. **The quote's journey.** It's one quoted line in D2, a split line ("YOU DONT —" / "— WANNA KNOW") in D4, also written whole in D4 as the formal "YOU DO NOT WANT TO KNOW," and finally spread across five places in D5, with "no no" added. Splitting it up was the last structural invention.
9. **The fondness changed hands.** D1: "that frown / Of that which you were so fond?" (the Minotaur is fond). D3: "THAT FROWN — OH YES — / YOU WERE SO FOND." D4: "OH, YES! YOU — NO / WE! … WERE ALL SO / SO VERY SO / FOND?" D5 keeps "YOU — NO — / WE." Over four drafts, the fondness moves from the beast to the speaker and the community. The "we" ending isn't decoration. It's what the revisions were heading toward.
10. **The parenthesis comes from a prayer.** D4 reads "NEVER LOST, [crossed out] WE HOPE WHILE WE PRAY," and D5 compresses it to "NEVER LOST (WE / HOPE." The open bracket is what's left of a crossed-out phrase, the aside "we hope while we pray" cut down and left open.
11. **Other cuts:** "dying tree" (D2), "EVER AFTER" (D4, a fairy-tale formula), "If wherefore: why" (D4, crossed out, though "wherefore" lives on in "FOR WHERE"), D3's long "GHOSTS CLEARED" passage, and D2's circular frame.

## The original panel, corrected

**The Prosodist: confirmed with a correction.** The four-syllable grid emerges in D4 and locks in D5, so it was built, not stumbled into. The correction: of the rhyme run I flagged (grace / place / embrace / face), grace, embrace, and face have been there since D1. Only "PLACE" is new in D5. If one rhyme goes, the drafts say it should be "PLACE." The cut is easy: D4 had "STOOD NO MATTER YOUR EMBRACE," and D5 could go back to "STOOD SIMPLE, WITHOUT / YOUR EMBRACE." On caps: they came from your notebook handwriting (D3, D4), not from a decision made while typing. D2 was all lowercase. The caps-and-lowercase split was only invented in D5, so "where do the caps break" is still a live choice, not a settled one.

**The Mythographer: partly overturned.** I read "HELL NOW / OR BE IT HELL / THEN" through Marlowe and Milton. The drafts show the line started in Rome ("seven hills") and reached hell by changing one vowel. The Marlowe and Milton echoes are real in D5, but they came late, and I missed the Rome layer completely. That layer matters: the Minotaur "roaming" the seven hills is a Cretan myth absorbed into Rome's empire of retold stories, which is mythologizing fact and factualizing myth one more time. The Evans/Knossos reading still holds, since the fact/myth idea is in D1 ("A myth that was true, a myth perused"). The drafts also show a grammar change I didn't credit: D4's "MYTHOLOGIZED FACT" (past participle, a history) became D5's "MYTHOLOGIZE / FACT" (imperative, a recipe). The stanza went from telling what happened to giving instructions.

**The Analyst: corrected on two points.**
1. I read "YOU — NO —" as an echo of the lowercase "no no." The drafts show it's a correction, "you — no, we," spelled out in D4 as "YOU — NO / WE!" It's still a breakdown, but the breakdown is a confession: the speaker takes the fondness back for themselves. That makes the psychic reading stronger.
2. I said the apostrophe drops out of "dont" as the voice wears down. D4 already had "YOU DONT —" in the first split version of the quote, so this is more likely a carry-over from the notebook than a planned erosion. Decide whether you want it to mean something.

I also argued the lowercase voice is the speaker's own repressed voice, not the Minotaur's. In D2, "you don't wanna know" comes straight after "poor minotaur, where are you now? / have you lost that frown…," so it reads as the Minotaur answering. Spreading it out in D5 makes the speaker ambiguous, and the drafts show that ambiguity was produced, not there from the start. Both readings are now available, and the poem is better for it.

**The Workshop Poet: two notes withdrawn.** "AND OR" survived D3, D4, and D5, and was added with a caret in D4. That's a choice, not a hedge, so I withdraw the cut. "'TWAS" is new in D5 and has no history, so the missing apostrophe is still probably a slip. New note: D4's three layout trials of the opening ("Walls / COLLAPSE JUST / JUST / SEALS," staggered) show you think of the page as space. That supports giving the quoted fragments' indentation a mapped path, and the opening stanzas could be staggered the same way.

**The Cold Reader: holds, with context.** The chiasmus stanza is still the most explicit moment in the poem, but the drafts show it's the most revised idea in the poem: a loose list in D1, a question in D3 ("WHAT IS A MYTH / TO DO?"), noun phrases in D2, participles in D4, imperatives in D5. Each version got shorter and more pointed. If it still reads like a slogan, the drafts suggest the fix isn't another rewrite. The fix is the line after it. "SINCE: ROAMED ALL" picks up the Rome layer, and leaning on "ROAM" a little harder would give the abstract stanza something to stand on.

## What the drafts show overall

The poem's main revision move is compression that leaves traces. A simile shrinks to a colon. A prayer shrinks to an open parenthesis. A stepping-stone and a proverb merge into one line. Rome turns into hell with one vowel. A whole sentence breaks into five pieces. In each case the earlier meaning stays under the new one. That's why the final version reads as layered: it is layered, by revision.

## Updated questions for the poet

1. Does "MINOS" in D3's margin mean the king or the Minotaur? If it's the king, he's the caps speaker.
2. Do you want the Rome layer ("seven hills") to stay audible, or is "ROAM" enough?
3. Is "PLACE" earning its spot, given that it's the only rhyme added in D5?
4. Was D2 a separate experiment you then grafted back in? Its circular frame ("where are you now?" at both ends) is the one structural idea that didn't survive. Was that on purpose?
